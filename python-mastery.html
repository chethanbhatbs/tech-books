Preface

There are, by recent count, more than three hundred books on the Python programming language in print. This one differs from most of them in two respects.

The first is that it does not begin with Python. It begins, instead, with the machine. For three chapters you will read no Python at all. You will read about transistors, bits, instructions, processes, and the long history of how human beings learnt to make machines obey them. This is not preamble. It is the foundation, without which every later chapter would be a story told from the middle.

The second is that it does not assume you wish to be told. It assumes you wish to understand. Where another book might say "use a list", this book will tell you what a list is in memory, why appending to it is fast and prepending is not, when to choose a tuple instead, and what CPython's implementers did to make the choice matter as little as possible. The aim is fluency, not just fluency in writing Python but fluency in thinking about it.

How to Use This Book

Read each chapter in order. The examples are numbered (Example 4.3 is the third example in Chapter 4); the exercises follow each chapter; the complete solutions appear at the end of every chapter. Do the exercises before reading the solutions, even when you are certain of the answer. Programming is a motor skill; the muscle memory of typing def and : at the right moment is built only by typing them.

On Depth and Further Reading

Every topic in this book is the subject of its own books, papers, and lifelong careers. We cannot, and will not pretend to, cover everything. Where a subject deserves a book of its own, you will find a "Further Reading" box at the end of the section, pointing to the resources we judge to be the best on the internet. This book is a foundation; those resources are where you continue building.

The Computer Beneath

Before you can program a machine, you should know what kind of machine it is. This chapter is a brief tour of the device, and of the five thousand years of effort that produced it, laying down only as much groundwork as the rest of the book will need.

A computer is a device which obeys instructions. We have built them so well that we now have to remember that this is all they do. — Maurice Wilkes, designer of EDSAC (1949), the first practical stored-program computer

The book you are holding teaches a programming language called Python. But Python is, in the end, a thin layer of human convenience laid over a very specific kind of machine, and that machine has properties (speed, capacity, behaviour) which constrain everything you will ever do with it. If you want to be a competent programmer, you can begin with the language and learn what is beneath only when you need to. If you want to be a thoughtful one, you begin with what is beneath. We are taking the second route.

This chapter is not a course in computer architecture. A proper course in computer architecture is, by itself, a year of university study; we point at the best ones in the Further Reading boxes throughout. What you will find here is the minimum that the rest of this book assumes: a vocabulary for the parts of a computer, an intuition for their relative speeds, and a sense of the long human history that led to the device on your desk.

1.0Before Electronics: Five Thousand Years of Calculating Machines

It is tempting, sitting in front of a glowing rectangle, to imagine that computing began with the silicon chip. It did not. The desire to delegate calculation to a machine is older than writing, and the lineage of devices that have answered that desire reaches back five thousand years. Knowing this lineage will not, by itself, make you a better Python programmer; but it will make you a less arrogant one, and that is its own kind of skill.

The Abacus, circa 2700 BCE

The abacus is the oldest surviving computing device. By moving beads along rods, a trained operator could perform addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and even, with practice, square roots, at speeds that competed with early electronic calculators well into the 1940s. The abacus encodes numbers positionally, which is the same principle on which every modern digital computer rests. It is also, in a small but real sense, a stored-program device: the position of the beads represents both the operand and the intermediate state of the calculation.

The Antikythera Mechanism, circa 100 BCE

For nineteen centuries the Antikythera mechanism sat in a chest of bronze fragments at the bottom of the Aegean. When it was finally CT-scanned in the 2000s, the world discovered that ancient Greece had possessed an analog computer: a hand-cranked geared device that predicted eclipses, tracked the Olympic Games on a four-year cycle, and modelled the apparent motion of five planets. There is nothing else like it from antiquity. Whatever industrial culture produced it left no other example, and the line of mechanical computing fell silent for a thousand years.

Pascal, Leibniz, and the Mechanical Calculators

In 1642, the nineteen-year-old Blaise Pascal built a mechanical calculator to help his father, a tax commissioner in Rouen, perform the arithmetic of public accounting. Three decades later, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz extended the idea with the stepped reckoner, which could multiply and divide. Leibniz also wrote, in 1703, the paper Explication de l'Arithmétique Binaire, in which he set out the binary number system, three hundred years before any device would exploit it.

Babbage, Lovelace, and the First Programs

In the 1830s, the English mathematician Charles Babbage designed a machine he called the Analytical Engine, a programmable, general-purpose mechanical computer driven by punched cards. He never finished it; the engineering tolerances of Victorian England were not equal to the task, and his money ran out. But the design was complete, and one of his collaborators, Ada Lovelace, the daughter of the poet Lord Byron, wrote a sequence of operations for the Engine to compute Bernoulli numbers. It was, by any reasonable definition, the first computer program, written for a machine that did not yet exist and would not for another century.

Lovelace also saw, more clearly than Babbage himself, what the device implied. Machines, she wrote, could in principle manipulate symbols, not just numbers, and might one day compose music or assist in any field "in which the relations of things susceptible of being expressed by abstract signs are involved." Every program you will ever write rests on the truth of that paragraph.

1.1The Universal Machine

In 1936, a twenty-four-year-old English mathematician named Alan Turing published a paper with the unpromising title On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem. It is among the most consequential documents ever written. In it, Turing described a device that did not exist (a mathematical fiction, a thought experiment) that could, given the right instructions, simulate any other computing device imaginable. He called it an "a-machine"; we now call it a Turing machine.

The device was preposterously simple. It consisted of an infinite tape divided into cells, a head that could read or write one symbol at a time, and a small table of rules of the form: if you see this symbol in this state, write that symbol, move left or right, and change state. From this minimum, Turing proved, you could compute anything that was computable at all.

The point is not the tape. The point is what Turing's argument established: that there exists a single kind of machine which, by being fed different instructions, can perform the work of any other kind of machine. This is the idea of the universal computer, and every device you own (phone, laptop, server, watch) is one of them. They differ only in speed, capacity, and what is plugged in.

History

The word computer originally referred to a person, usually a woman, whose job was to perform calculations by hand. NASA's "human computers", including Katherine Johnson, Dorothy Vaughan, and Mary Jackson, continued to work into the 1970s. The machine took the name from the role it replaced.

1.2The von Neumann Architecture

In June 1945, the Hungarian-American polymath John von Neumann circulated a draft document, the First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC, describing the architecture of a computer that would store its program in the same memory as its data. This was a departure from the practice of the time, in which programs were entered by physically rewiring the machine. Once a program lived in memory, it could be modified, copied, transmitted, and executed without altering a single wire. The idea spread quickly, and within five years almost every computer being built used the design. We still call it the von Neumann architecture.

The architecture has four functional parts. The CPU is where computation happens. It contains an arithmetic logic unit for arithmetic and bitwise operations, a control unit for fetching and decoding instructions, and a small set of very fast working slots called registers. The memory (RAM, for random-access memory) holds programs and data; it is much larger than the registers but much slower. Storage (solid-state and rotating disks) holds data permanently across power cycles. I/O devices are the senses and limbs by which the machine reaches the world.

The CPU runs in a continuous loop, called the fetch-decode-execute cycle. It reads the instruction at the address held in a special register called the instruction pointer, decodes what it asks for, performs it, advances the pointer, and repeats. A modern processor performs this loop several billion times per second. The instruction at one address might be "add register A to register B"; the next might be "store the result at memory location 0x100"; the next might be "if register A is zero, jump to address 0x500". Out of these primitive moves, executed in sequence, every program runs.

Clock Cycle

The basic unit of time inside a CPU. A 3 GHz processor has three billion cycles per second; one cycle takes about 0.33 nanoseconds. A simple instruction may complete in one cycle; a complex one (a multiplication, a memory load) may take several. The number of cycles per instruction is called the CPI, and modern CPUs use pipelining and out-of-order execution to bring it below 1 in favourable code.

Example 1.1A single instruction, in three notations

Consider the operation: add the integer in register RBX to the integer in register RAX. Here it is, in three forms, descending toward what the silicon actually sees:

; assembly (what humans wrote in 1955)
ADD RAX, RBX
# machine code (what the CPU reads)
48 01 D8
# the bits, in binary (what is on the wire)
01001000 00000001 11011000

The three lines describe the same operation. Every program in your computer's memory is, ultimately, a sequence of bytes like the second line. Assembly is a one-to-one human-readable transcription of it. Python is many, many layers above.

1.3The Memory Hierarchy

Memory in a modern computer is not one thing. It is a hierarchy of several layers, each faster and smaller than the next. Understanding this hierarchy, and the orders of magnitude that separate its levels, is the difference between writing programs that are fast and programs that pretend to be.

To grasp these numbers, multiply each by ten billion to scale the timings into human ones. A register access becomes about 3 seconds. An L1 cache access becomes 10 seconds. An L2 / L3 access becomes a minute and a half. A RAM access becomes about 15 minutes. An SSD read becomes a little over a day. A network round-trip becomes about a month. This is the world your program lives in. When people say a Python program is "slow", they almost always mean it is spending too much time at the lower levels of this pyramid, waiting for memory, for disk, for the network, and not enough time near the top.

Performance Intuition

If a program runs a loop of arithmetic over values already in cache, it can manage billions of operations per second. If the same loop has to fetch each value from RAM, the rate drops by a factor of 100. If it has to read each value from SSD, the rate drops by another factor of 1,000. The size of these gaps is why so much modern software design is, in the end, about locality: keeping the data near the work.

1.4Numbers in Binary

The CPU only knows numbers, and even those it represents in binary, a positional notation with base 2 instead of base 10. Each digit in a binary number is called a bit and takes the value 0 or 1. Eight bits form a byte, which can represent 28 = 256 distinct values.

The shift from decimal to binary is mostly a mechanical translation. The decimal number 13, written 1101 in binary, decomposes as:

  1 × 2³  +  1 × 2²  +  0 × 2¹  +  1 × 2⁰
=    8    +    4     +    0     +    1
=   13

Integers: Two's Complement

Storing negative numbers is the first complication. Almost every modern CPU uses a scheme called two's complement. In an 8-bit two's-complement representation, the leftmost bit signals sign (0 for positive, 1 for negative), but the negative values are not simply the positives with the sign flipped. Instead, to negate a number, you invert all the bits and add 1.

Why this strange-looking scheme? Because under two's complement, the CPU can use a single circuit, the addition circuit, to perform both addition and subtraction. Computing 5 - 3 is mechanically identical to computing 5 + (-3). The cost of this elegance is a small asymmetry: an 8-bit two's-complement integer ranges from -128 to +127, not -127 to +127. One negative number has no positive partner.

Example 1.2Integer overflow, in C and in Python

Most languages treat an integer as a fixed-size container. C's int is typically 32 bits, and what happens when you exceed its range is among the most quietly devastating bugs in programming history:

// C
int a = 2147483647;     // 2^31 - 1, the maximum 32-bit signed int
int b = a + 1;
printf("%d", b);          // prints -2147483648  (wraps around!)

The 1996 explosion of the Ariane 5 rocket, which destroyed €500 million of European satellites, was caused by exactly this kind of overflow: a 64-bit floating-point value was converted into a 16-bit signed integer that could not hold it. Python's design rejects this trap entirely. Its integers grow as large as needed:

# Python
a = 2 ** 1000
print(len(str(a)))         # 302: the number has 302 digits
print(a + 1 != a)         # True: no overflow, ever
302
True

This is a trade. C's fixed-size integers are dramatically faster to manipulate; Python's are dramatically safer. You will, in your career, have reason to prefer each.

Real Numbers: IEEE 754

Storing non-integer numbers (3.14, 0.001, 6.022 × 1023) requires a scheme more elaborate than counting in binary. The standard used by essentially every CPU built since the late 1980s is IEEE 754. It represents a real number as a triple: a sign bit, an exponent, and a fraction (also called the mantissa or significand).

The consequence of this representation is a fact every programmer must internalise: floating-point numbers are approximations. The decimal 0.1 cannot be expressed exactly in binary, much as 1/3 cannot be expressed exactly in decimal. The closest 64-bit IEEE 754 approximation of 0.1 is, in fact:

0.1000000000000000055511151231257827021181583404541015625

This tiny error, harmless in isolation, accumulates over millions of operations and reveals itself in unexpected places. We will return to the consequences in Chapter 4. For now, internalise the diagram and the principle.

1.5Machine Code, Assembly, and the First Bug

A program in memory is a sequence of bytes interpreted as instructions. Each instruction tells the CPU to do one of a few hundred primitive operations: load a value, store a value, add, subtract, compare, jump. The encoding is determined by the CPU's instruction set architecture: x86-64 for most laptops and desktops, ARM64 for phones and recent Macs, RISC-V for an increasing slice of embedded and research hardware.

Direct programming in machine code was abandoned by the late 1950s. Programmers wrote in assembly, a near-perfect one-to-one human-readable transcription of machine code, until the high-level languages of the 1960s displaced it for most work. Today, assembly remains in use only for the innermost loops of operating-system kernels, drivers, and certain performance-critical libraries.

Example 1.3A C function and its assembly

This is the C function add:

int add(int a, int b) {
    return a + b;
}

The x86-64 assembly it compiles to under optimisation looks like this:

add:
    lea     eax, [rdi + rsi]   ; eax = rdi + rsi
    ret                        ; return; result is in eax

Two instructions. The C function's arguments arrive in registers rdi and rsi (per the System V calling convention), the sum is placed in eax, and ret hands control back to the caller. The equivalent Python function, also a one-liner, runs through perhaps two hundred machine instructions on the interpreter side before the addition itself is performed.

The First Bug

The Call Stack

When a function calls another function, the CPU must remember where it was so it can come back. The mechanism is the call stack: a region of memory that grows downward as functions are entered and shrinks upward as they return. Each function's local variables, parameters, and return address live in a region of the stack called its stack frame.

Python, too, has a call stack. When an exception is raised, the traceback it prints is, literally, a snapshot of this stack at the moment of the error. We will see this again in Chapter 21.

1.6Processes, Threads, and the Operating System

Real computers do not run one program at a time. They run dozens, sometimes hundreds: your editor, your web browser, your music player, the daemons that keep your network alive, the system that updates your clock. The illusion that each program owns the machine is sustained by a piece of software called the operating system.

An operating system (Windows, macOS, Linux, iOS, Android) does three principal things. It multiplexes the CPU, slicing time into small intervals, running a different program in each, switching between them faster than any human perception can detect. It isolates programs from one another, giving each its own private view of memory, called a virtual address space, so that one program's bug cannot corrupt another's data. And it mediates access to hardware: your program does not write directly to the screen or read from the network card; it asks the operating system, which checks permissions and routes the request.

Process

A running instance of a program, as seen by the operating system. A process has its own memory space, its own file descriptors, its own permissions, and at least one thread of execution. When you launch Python, the OS creates a Python process. When you launch a second Python interpreter, you get a second, completely independent process.

Thread

A path of execution within a process. A single process may have many threads, sharing the same memory but each with its own call stack and its own current instruction pointer. Threads are how a program does several things in parallel. They are also a notorious source of bugs, because shared memory plus parallel execution is a difficult combination to reason about.

Python has a famously specific relationship with threads: the Global Interpreter Lock (GIL) ensures that only one thread inside a CPython interpreter executes Python bytecode at a time, even on a multi-core machine. This trade, simplicity of the interpreter's internals at the cost of CPU-parallelism, has been a subject of debate for thirty years. We will look at it properly in Chapter 21. For now, just know it is there.

1.7What Python Inherits From All This

The Python you write does not, in any literal sense, run on the machine described above. It runs on a virtual machine (a piece of C code called the CPython interpreter) which itself runs on the machine described above. But the constraints of the underlying hardware leak through. When you create a list of a million integers, those million PyObject pointers occupy real bytes in real memory. When you call a function, a real stack frame is pushed onto a real stack. When your program is slow, it is slow for reasons rooted in the hierarchy of Figure 1.10.

A programmer who knows none of this can still write Python. They will, however, be regularly puzzled. Why is appending to a list fast but inserting at the front slow? Why is dictionary lookup constant-time but list in linear? Why does multiprocessing exist when threading already does? Each answer involves the hardware. We will encounter all three before this book is finished, and you will understand each immediately, because you read this chapter.

The remaining chapters of Part I cover the second half of the foundation: where Python itself came from, what CPython actually is, and how a Python statement becomes electricity. After that the language itself begins.

Solutions to Chapter 1 Exercises

Solution 1.1: Bit counting

1 bit gives 21 = 2 values (0 or 1). 4 bits give 24 = 16. 16 bits give 216 = 65,536. 64 bits give 264 ≈ 1.8 × 1019, about 18 quintillion. A 64-bit integer can count every grain of sand on Earth and have room to spare; it can count the seconds since the universe began with about 22 orders of magnitude unused. The only domains that need more are mathematics (cryptography, combinatorics) and certain scientific simulations.

Solution 1.2: Two's complement of −37

+37  =  00100101
invert: 11011010
add 1:  11011011

verify: 00100101    (+37)
      + 11011011    (−37)
      = 00000000    (carry discarded)

So −37 in 8-bit two's complement is 11011011 (decimal 219 if interpreted as unsigned).

Solution 1.3: Memory hierarchy scaling

The ratio is 0.1 / (0.3 × 10−9) ≈ 3.3 × 108, about a third of a billion times slower. Scaled so a register access is 1 second, a network round-trip is roughly 333 million seconds, which is about 10.5 years. This is why caching matters so much: the cost of going to the network for data you already have nearby is, on this scale, geological.

Solution 1.4: Why 0.1 is not 0.1

A binary fraction terminates only if the denominator (when the fraction is in lowest terms) is a power of 2. The fraction 1/10 = 1/(2 × 5) has a factor of 5 in its denominator, so it does not terminate in binary; it produces a repeating expansion 0.000110011001100… that must be truncated to fit in 52 mantissa bits. Decimals that do terminate include 0.5 (= 1/2), 0.25 (= 1/4), 0.125 (= 1/8), and any sum of these. So 0.5 + 0.25 = 0.75 is exact in a float; 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.3 is not.

Solution 1.5: Stack overflow

1 MB / 200 B = 5,242 frames at most. Python's default limit of 1000 is set well below the hardware ceiling, deliberately, to give a clearer error message before the C stack itself overflows, which would crash the interpreter outright. The limit can be raised with sys.setrecursionlimit(), but doing so without an architectural reason is usually a code smell. Most deep recursions are better rewritten as loops.

Solution 1.6: An instruction budget

3 × 109 × 0.1 = 3 × 108 instructions per page render, three hundred million. This is for one core; modern machines have several. The number sounds enormous and is. The amount of work a modern browser does to lay out a page (parsing HTML, resolving CSS, computing layout, painting pixels, running JavaScript) comfortably fills it.

Solution 1.7: Processes vs threads

A process is a running program with its own private memory space; an operating system isolates each from the others. A thread is a path of execution within a single process; multiple threads in one process share memory directly. Threads are preferable when tasks need to communicate constantly (a web server handing requests off to worker threads that share a connection pool). Processes are preferable when tasks must not be able to corrupt each other (a browser's tab-per-process model, where a crash in one tab does not bring down the others).

Solution 1.8: Ariane 5

The Ariane 5 inherited inertial reference software from Ariane 4. In a routine pre-flight conversion, a 64-bit floating-point measurement of horizontal velocity was cast into a 16-bit signed integer. Ariane 5 was a faster rocket; the velocity exceeded 32,767 (the maximum value the 16-bit integer could hold); the conversion overflowed and produced a value the guidance system interpreted as garbage. The rocket veered, broke up under aerodynamic stress, and self-destructed 37 seconds after launch. The original Ariane 4 conversion had been deemed safe based on physical limits the older rocket could not exceed; nobody re-verified that assumption when the software was carried over. The fault was not a bug in the conversion code. The fault was the absence of a check on the assumption beneath it.

The Lineage of Python

No language is invented from nothing. Python is the considered descendant of half a dozen earlier languages, the personal project of one programmer, and the collective work of thousands since. This chapter traces where it came from and explains what actually happens inside the program that runs your code.

I decided to try to design a language that I myself wanted to use. I had no illusions that this would change the world, but I knew I wanted to scratch my own itch. — Guido van Rossum, creator of Python

In December 1989, a thirty-three-year-old Dutch programmer named Guido van Rossum found himself with a week of free time over the Christmas holiday and a problem he had been turning over for years. His office at the Centrum Wiskunde & Informatica in Amsterdam was closed. He had a home computer and an idea: a scripting language that would have the power of the systems languages he used at work, but the gentleness of a teaching language he had helped build in the 1980s. He started writing an interpreter. He named the project after a British comedy troupe whose scripts he had been reading, Monty Python's Flying Circus. The snake came later, and only as a logo.

The language that grew out of that holiday is now, by most measures, the most widely taught and most widely written programming language in the world. To understand why it has the shape it does, you have to understand the languages it descended from, the decisions its creator made deliberately, and the machinery that the standard implementation, CPython, uses to bring your code to life. That is the work of this chapter.

2.1A Family of Languages

By the time Python was conceived, programming languages had been evolving for more than thirty years, and a recognisable family tree had formed. Each new language was, in large part, a reaction to the frustrations of the ones before it. Knowing the broad shape of that tree explains a surprising amount about why Python looks the way it does.

The first high-level language to see wide use was FORTRAN (1957), built by a team under John Backus at IBM. Its great contribution was the compiler: a program whose job was to translate something resembling mathematical notation into machine code automatically, so that programmers no longer had to write the machine code themselves. Many doubted a compiler could ever produce code efficient enough to be worth using. Backus proved them wrong, and in doing so invented an entire category of software.

The languages that followed each added an idea. LISP (1958) introduced the notion that code and data could share the same form, and brought recursion and automatic memory management into the mainstream. ALGOL (1960) gave us block structure and the lexical scope rules that nearly every later language, including Python, would adopt. Simula (1967) invented the object: a bundle of data together with the procedures that act on it. C (1972) struck a balance between closeness to the hardware and human readability so successful that it became, and remains, the language in which operating systems are written. Smalltalk (1972) took Simula's object idea to its logical conclusion, making everything in the language an object, an idea Python would later embrace wholeheartedly.

2.2The Three Parents of Python

Python did not descend evenly from the whole tree. Three languages shaped it directly, and each left a different mark.

ABC: the gift of readability

Through most of the 1980s, Guido van Rossum worked at CWI on a language called ABC, intended to teach programming to beginners and to be usable by scientists who were not professional programmers. ABC was where Python's most distinctive feature was born: the use of indentation, rather than braces or keywords, to mark which statements belong together. ABC also favoured a small number of powerful, high-level data types and an uncluttered syntax that read almost like English.

ABC ultimately failed to find an audience, and Guido came to understand why. It was a closed world. You could not easily add new features, talk to the operating system, or read and write ordinary files. It tried to be a complete environment rather than a tool that cooperated with the rest of the system. When Guido designed Python, he kept ABC's readability and threw out its isolation. Python would be extensible from the start, and it would talk to everything.

C: the language Python is written in

The standard Python interpreter is a program written in C, and this is no accident. C was the lingua franca of systems programming, the language in which the operating systems Python needed to run on were themselves built. Writing the interpreter in C meant Python could run anywhere C ran, which by 1990 meant almost everywhere. It also meant that performance-critical pieces of Python, and later entire libraries such as NumPy, could be written in C and called from Python, giving the language a way to escape its own slowness when it mattered. We will return to this idea repeatedly; it is central to why a "slow" language powers so much fast software.

Modula-3: modules and exceptions

While at CWI, Guido encountered Modula-3, a language from the Digital Equipment Corporation research labs. From it Python borrowed its module system, the way code is organised into importable units, and the basic shape of its exception handling, the try and except machinery you will meet in Chapter 21. These were mature, well-considered designs, and rather than reinvent them Guido adapted what already worked.

Scripting Language

A language designed for writing short programs that automate tasks, glue other programs together, or configure systems, rather than for building large standalone applications from scratch. The term has blurred over time: Python began as a scripting language and is now used to build systems of every size. The distinction was always more about intent than capability.

2.3Guido's Design Decisions

A language is the sum of thousands of small choices, and the choices that give Python its character were made early and held to with unusual consistency. Four of them matter most.

The first is readability above cleverness. Guido observed that code is read far more often than it is written: a line written once may be read a hundred times over the life of a program, by its author and by strangers. He optimised the language for the reader. This is why indentation is mandatory rather than decorative, why the language avoids cryptic punctuation, and why there is a cultural preference, captured in the Zen of Python, for there to be one obvious way to do a thing rather than many clever ones.

The second is that everything is an object. Numbers, strings, functions, classes, modules, even the types themselves: all are objects, with the same uniform interface. This decision, inherited from Smalltalk, gives the language a deep consistency. The whole of Chapter 3 is devoted to its consequences.

The third is batteries included. Python ships with a large standard library so that common tasks, reading files, parsing dates, fetching web pages, working with JSON, can be done out of the box without hunting for third-party code. This made Python immediately useful and helped it spread.

The fourth is pragmatism over purity. Where a purely object-oriented language insists you write list.length(), Python offers the blunter len(list) because it reads better. Where a purely functional language forbids changing data in place, Python allows it where it is convenient. Python borrows good ideas from every paradigm and refuses to be a slave to any one of them.

2.5The Compilation Pipeline

Python is often called an interpreted language, and it is, but the description hides something. Before any interpretation happens, your source code passes through several stages of translation. Understanding these stages demystifies a great deal of the interpreter's behaviour, including why syntax errors are reported before your program runs at all, and what those __pycache__ folders contain.

The tokenizer reads your source as a stream of characters and groups them into tokens: the name x, the operator =, the number 2, and so on. The parser arranges those tokens into a tree that reflects the grammatical structure of the program, called the Abstract Syntax Tree. Since version 3.9, CPython uses a PEG parser, a more powerful design than the one it replaced. The compiler walks that tree and emits bytecode, a sequence of simple instructions for a virtual machine. Finally, the virtual machine, a loop in a C file historically named ceval.c, executes the bytecode one instruction at a time.

Example 2.2Looking at the bytecode

The dis module ("disassemble") lets you see the bytecode the compiler produced for any function. This is not a trick; it is the actual sequence the virtual machine will run.

import dis

def add(a, b):
    return a + b

dis.dis(add)
  2           RESUME                   0
  3           LOAD_FAST                a
              LOAD_FAST                b
              BINARY_OP                0 (+)
              RETURN_VALUE

Five instructions. LOAD_FAST pushes a local variable onto an internal stack, BINARY_OP pops two values and pushes their sum, and RETURN_VALUE hands the result back. The Python virtual machine is a stack machine: nearly every instruction works by pushing values onto, or popping them off, an operand stack.

Bytecode

An intermediate, low-level instruction set, simpler than Python but higher-level than machine code, that the Python virtual machine executes. It is portable across operating systems and CPUs, which machine code is not. The .pyc files in __pycache__ are compiled bytecode, cached so that importing an unchanged module skips recompilation. They are an optimisation, safe to delete; Python regenerates them as needed.

2.6Reference Counting and the Garbage Collector

In a language like C, the programmer must explicitly free every piece of memory they allocate. Forgetting to do so is a memory leak; freeing memory still in use is a use-after-free, the source of a great share of the security vulnerabilities of the last forty years. Python relieves you of this burden entirely. You never free memory by hand. Understanding how it does this for you is worth a few pages, because it explains some otherwise mysterious behaviour.

CPython's primary mechanism is reference counting. Every object carries a hidden counter recording how many names, list slots, or other references currently point at it. Each new reference increments the count; each reference that goes away decrements it. The instant the count reaches zero, the object is unreachable, and its memory is reclaimed immediately.

Reference counting is simple and prompt, but it has one blind spot: cycles. If object A holds a reference to object B, and B holds a reference back to A, then even after every outside name to them is gone, each still has a reference count of one, pointing at the other. Neither will ever be freed by counting alone. To catch these, CPython runs a second mechanism, a generational cyclic garbage collector, which periodically looks for groups of objects that refer only to each other and are reachable from nothing else, and reclaims them.

Why two mechanisms

Reference counting handles the overwhelming majority of objects instantly and predictably. The cyclic collector exists only to mop up the rare cycles that counting cannot. Splitting the work this way keeps the common case fast. You can inspect and even tune the cyclic collector through the gc module, though you will rarely need to.

2.7The Global Interpreter Lock

No discussion of CPython is complete without the Global Interpreter Lock, universally abbreviated the GIL. It is the single most debated design decision in the language's history, and the one beginners most often hear about without understanding.

The GIL is a mutex, a lock, that ensures only one thread executes Python bytecode at any given moment, even on a processor with many cores. The reason it exists is reference counting. Recall that every object's reference count is constantly being incremented and decremented. If two threads tried to adjust the same count at the same time, the count could be corrupted, leading to objects freed too early or never freed at all. The GIL makes the interpreter's internals safe by the simplest possible means: it lets only one thread run Python code at a time, so the counts can never be touched concurrently.

The consequence is precise and worth stating carefully. For work that is input-output bound, programs that spend most of their time waiting for the network, the disk, or a database, threads work well, because a thread that is waiting releases the GIL for others to use. But for work that is CPU bound, pure calculation, Python threads cannot run on more than one core at once, and adding threads buys you nothing. The traditional answer has been to use multiple processes instead, each with its own interpreter and its own GIL, communicating through channels the operating system provides. This is the subject of Chapter 21.

After three decades of debate, this is now changing. A 2023 proposal, PEP 703, laid out a way to make CPython work correctly without the GIL, and recent versions ship an experimental "free-threaded" build that removes it. Making reference counting safe without a single global lock is delicate work, and the free-threaded build is still maturing, but the long era in which the GIL was simply a fact of Python life is drawing toward its close.

Solution 2.4: Watch a reference count

import sys
a = [1, 2, 3]
print(sys.getrefcount(a))   # 2: the name a, plus getrefcount's own argument
b = a
print(sys.getrefcount(a))   # 3: a, b, and the argument
del b
print(sys.getrefcount(a))   # 2 again

When you call getrefcount(a), the value a is passed as an argument to that function, which means that during the call there is one extra reference to the object: the function's parameter. So the reported count is always one higher than the number of references in your own code. This is a small but perfect illustration of the principle that, in Python, passing a value to a function creates another reference to it.

Solution 2.5: Reason about the GIL

Program A (downloading 100 web pages) is input-output bound: most of its time is spent waiting for the network. A waiting thread releases the GIL, so other threads can run while it waits. Threads would speed this up significantly. Program B (factoring large numbers) is CPU bound: it is pure computation with no waiting. Because the GIL allows only one thread to execute Python bytecode at a time, the threads would take turns on a single core and the 8 cores would sit mostly idle. Threads would not help; you would use multiple processes (or a free-threaded build) instead.

Solution 2.6: Trace the pipeline

When you press Enter, the operating system starts a CPython process and hands it hello.py. The tokenizer reads the file's characters and groups them into tokens. The parser arranges those tokens into an Abstract Syntax Tree representing the program's structure; if the syntax is invalid, you get a SyntaxError here, before anything runs. The compiler walks the tree and emits bytecode, which may be cached as a .pyc file. The virtual machine then executes the bytecode one instruction at a time, and when it reaches a print call, output appears on your screen through the operating system's input-output services.

Solution 2.7: Find a PEP

PEP 572 added assignment expressions, written :=, which let you bind a value to a name in the middle of a larger expression, for instance inside a while condition or a comprehension, so the value need not be computed twice or assigned on a separate line. Solution 2.8: A research question

A just-in-time (JIT) compiler watches a program as it runs, identifies the "hot" code that executes most often (typically loops), and compiles that code to native machine instructions on the fly, so subsequent runs of that code skip interpretation entirely and run at near-C speed. It can also specialise the machine code to the actual types it observes at runtime. CPython interprets each bytecode instruction every time, paying the interpretation cost on every pass through a loop. By not having a JIT, CPython stays far simpler: easier to maintain, easier to embed in other programs, and far easier to extend with C libraries, which is the trade its designers chose. (Recent CPython versions have begun adding a limited JIT of their own, narrowing the gap.)

The Object Model

Here Python truly begins. Before the first number or string, there is one idea from which the entire language unfolds: everything you can name is an object, and every object has the same three properties. Grasp this chapter and the rest of the book becomes a series of corollaries.

Everything is an object. The big idea is messaging. The key in making great and growable systems is much more to design how its modules communicate rather than what their internal properties and behaviors should be. — Alan Kay, who coined the term "object-oriented programming"

Most introductions to Python begin with numbers and strings, treating them as different kinds of thing with different rules, and only much later, if at all, reveal that they were the same kind of thing all along. We are going to do the opposite. We will spend this chapter on the single idea that unifies the whole language, and only then, in the chapters that follow, look at the particular objects it gives you. The order is unusual, but it is the order that makes everything afterward simple. When you understand that a number, a string, a list, a function, and a class are all objects obeying one set of rules, you stop memorising special cases and start reasoning from principle.

The promise of this chapter is concrete. By its end you will understand exactly what a variable is (not what most beginners think), why two equal-looking values are sometimes the same object and sometimes not, why changing a list inside a function changes it outside but changing a number does not, why a list cannot be a dictionary key, and how the + sign knows how to add two numbers and also join two strings. These are not five facts to memorise. They are five consequences of one idea.

3.1Everything Is an Object

In some languages, the world is divided in two. There are primitives, the small fast things like integers and characters that the hardware understands directly, and there are objects, the larger structured things you build yourself. The two obey different rules. A primitive cannot have methods; an object can. This division is a constant low tax on the programmer's attention, who must always remember which kind of thing they are holding.

Python abolishes the division. In Python, everything is an object: the integer 42, the string "hello", the list [1, 2, 3], the function you define, the class you write, the module you import, and even the types themselves. Every one of them is an object with the same uniform interface. This is why you can ask an integer for its methods, store a function in a list, pass a class as an argument, and treat all of them with the same handful of built-in functions.

Example 3.1Asking different things the same questions

The built-in type() reports any object's type, and type() works on everything, because everything is an object:

type(42)            # <class 'int'>
type("hello")       # <class 'str'>
type([1, 2, 3])     # <class 'list'>
type(print)         # <class 'builtin_function_or_method'>
type(int)           # <class 'type'>, even a type is an object
type(type)          # <class 'type'>, and its type is type itself

The last two lines deserve a pause. The type of int is type. The type of type is type. The hierarchy does not regress forever; it closes on itself. type is the object whose job is to make other types, and it is its own type. This is the still point at the centre of the whole model.

A practical consequence is that functions and classes are first-class: they can be assigned to names, stored in containers, passed as arguments, and returned from other functions, exactly like any other value. Much of Python's expressive power, and the whole of Chapter 16 on decorators, rests on this.

3.2Identity, Type, and Value

Every object in Python, without exception, has exactly three intrinsic properties. Understanding them, and which of them can change, resolves most of the confusions that follow.

The identity is what distinguishes an object from every other object that exists at the same time. It is fixed the moment the object is created and never changes for as long as the object lives. You can read it with the built-in id(). In CPython, the identity is simply the object's address in memory. The operator is compares identities: a is b asks whether a and b are the very same object, not merely equal ones.

The type determines what an object is and therefore what it can do: what operations it supports, what methods it has, how it behaves. Like identity, an object's type is fixed at creation. You read it with type(). The type is itself an object, as we saw in Section 3.1.

The value is the data the object holds. Here is the one property that may change: whether it can depends entirely on the object's type. Types whose values can change after creation are called mutable; types whose values cannot are called immutable. This single distinction, the subject of Section 3.4, is the most consequential fact about any Python object.

Identity

The property that uniquely distinguishes an object during its lifetime, returned by id(obj) and compared by the is operator. Two objects with identical values may still have different identities. In CPython, identity is the object's memory address. Do not rely on the specific number; rely only on whether two identities are equal.

3.3Names Are Not Boxes

We now reach the single most important correction this book can offer a beginner. In school mathematics, and in many programming languages, a variable is a box: x = 5 opens a box labelled x and puts a 5 inside it. Assigning x = 7 replaces the contents of the box. This mental model is wrong for Python, and the difference is not academic; it is the source of more genuine confusion than any other feature of the language.

In Python, a variable is not a box. It is a name, and a name is a label that is bound to an object that exists elsewhere. The statement x = 5 does three things, in order: it creates (or finds) an integer object with the value 5, it creates the name x, and it binds the name to the object, like tying a luggage tag to a suitcase. The name is not the object. The name is a way to reach the object.

Because a name is only a label, several names can be bound to one object at once. This is called aliasing, and it is the source of a beginner's first truly baffling bug. When you write b = a, you do not copy the object; you bind a second name to it. If the object is mutable and you change it through one name, the change is visible through the other, because there was only ever one object.

Example 3.2Aliasing, and how to avoid it

The shared-object behaviour, and the three usual ways to ask for a genuine copy:

a = [1, 2, 3]
b = a               # b is bound to the SAME list
b.append(4)
print(a)            # [1, 2, 3, 4], a sees it too
print(a is b)       # True, one object, two names

# To get an independent copy, ask for one explicitly:
c = a.copy()        # or  a[:]  or  list(a)
c.append(5)
print(a)            # [1, 2, 3, 4], unchanged
print(a is c)       # False, different objects

A shallow copy duplicates the outer list but not the objects inside it. If your list contains other mutable objects (lists of lists), you need copy.deepcopy() to copy all the way down. We return to this in Chapter 8.

Reassignment fits the same model. When you write x = 7 after x = 5, you do not change the integer 5 into a 7. You bind the name x to a different object, the integer 7. The 5 is untouched; if nothing else refers to it, its reference count drops to zero and it is reclaimed, exactly as Chapter 2 described.

3.4Mutability

An object is mutable if its value can change after it is created, and immutable if it cannot. This property belongs to the type, not the individual object: every list is mutable, every string is immutable, and no exceptions exist. The table below sorts the built-in types.

The crucial subtlety is what it means to "change" an immutable object: you cannot, and any operation that appears to is in fact creating a new object and rebinding your name to it. When you write s = s.upper(), the string method upper() does not alter the original string; it builds and returns a new one, and the assignment binds s to that new string. The original is unchanged, and if no other name holds it, it is reclaimed.

The classic trap

Because mutability is a property of the type, a mutable object used as a function's default argument is created once, when the function is defined, and then shared across every call. This produces one of the most surprising bugs in the language. We cover it in full in Chapter 15, but it is, at root, simply this section's principle applied to a default value.

3.5What Happens When You Pass an Object to a Function

Programmers arriving from other languages often ask whether Python passes arguments "by value" or "by reference". The honest answer is that it does neither, in the sense those terms usually carry, and the confusion dissolves the moment you apply the model of names and objects you already have. Python passes arguments by binding the parameter name to the same object the caller passed. The name inside the function and the name outside it are two labels on one object. Some call this "call by object reference" or "call by sharing".

From this single rule, the behaviour that surprises people follows necessarily. If the object is mutable and the function mutates it in place, the caller sees the change, because there is one object. If the function instead rebinds its parameter to a new object, the caller sees nothing, because rebinding a local name has no effect on the caller's name.

There is nothing special about functions here. The parameter is simply a new name, bound by the call to the argument object, in exactly the way b = a binds a new name in ordinary code. Once you see function calls as binding, you never again have to memorise a separate rule for them.

3.6Equality versus Identity

Two questions can be asked of a pair of objects, and beginners frequently confuse them. Are these the same object? is a question of identity, answered by is. Do these objects have the same value? is a question of equality, answered by ==. They are different questions with sometimes different answers, and using the wrong operator is a common and subtle bug.

a = [1, 2, 3]
b = [1, 2, 3]    # a separate list with equal contents
c = a

a == b      # True, same value
a is b      # False, different objects
a is c      # True, same object

The rule of thumb is short. Use == to compare values, which is what you want almost all the time. Use is only to ask about identity, and in practice that means almost exclusively comparing against None, which is a unique object: write if x is None, never if x == None. The reason is None is preferred is both speed and correctness, since a perverse class could define == to lie, but nothing can fake identity with the one true None.

One quirk of CPython tempts beginners into misusing is, and it is worth seeing so that you are never fooled by it. For efficiency, CPython keeps a single cached copy of every small integer, from -5 to 256, and reuses it. So two names set to 100 happen to point at the same cached object, and is returns True. But two names set to 1000 get separate objects, and is returns False. The values are equal in both cases; the identities are an implementation detail you must never depend on.

3.7Hashability

Some objects can serve as dictionary keys or set members, and some cannot. The dividing property is called hashability. An object is hashable if it has a hash value that never changes during its lifetime, provided by a method called __hash__, and if it can be compared for equality with other objects. Dictionaries and sets use this hash value to find things quickly, a mechanism we explain fully in Chapter 10.

The practical rule connects directly to mutability. Immutable built-in objects, numbers, strings, tuples (of hashable things), and frozensets, are hashable and may be used as keys. Mutable built-in objects, lists, dictionaries, and sets, are not hashable and may not. The reason is a matter of necessity, not arbitrary rule: if you could change an object after using it as a key, its hash would change, and the dictionary would lose track of where it stored the entry.

d = {}
d[(1, 2)] = "a point"      # tuple key: fine, tuples are hashable
d["name"] = "Ada"          # str key: fine

d[[1, 2]] = "oops"         # TypeError: unhashable type: 'list'

hash((1, 2))                # a fixed integer, e.g. -3550055125485641917
hash([1, 2])                # TypeError: unhashable type: 'list'
The contract

Hashability ties together equality and hashing by one rule: if two objects are equal, they must have the same hash. Objects you design yourself inherit a sensible default, but if you override __eq__ to define your own notion of equality, you must override __hash__ to match, or your objects will misbehave as dictionary keys. Chapter 19 covers how to do this correctly.

3.8The Data Model: How Objects Speak a Common Language

We can now answer the question posed at the start of the chapter: how does the + sign know how to add two numbers and also join two strings and also concatenate two lists? The answer reveals the machinery beneath the whole language. Python defines a set of special methods, recognisable by their double-underscore names and universally nicknamed dunder methods, that objects implement to participate in the language's syntax. When you write a + b, Python does not have special knowledge of addition. It calls a.__add__(b), and the object decides what addition means for its type.

This is the deepest reason "everything is an object" is more than a slogan. The syntax of the language is uniform precisely because it is all implemented as method calls on objects, and an object of any type, including one you write yourself, can implement those methods and thereby behave like a built-in. Example 3.4A type that behaves like a built-in

By implementing the right dunder methods, a class you write supports the same syntax as any built-in. Here a two-dimensional vector that can be added with + and printed legibly:

class Vector:
    def __init__(self, x, y):
        self.x, self.y = x, y

    def __add__(self, other):
        return Vector(self.x + other.x, self.y + other.y)

    def __repr__(self):
        return f"Vector({self.x}, {self.y})"

v = Vector(1, 2) + Vector(3, 4)
print(v)            # Vector(4, 6)

The + worked on a type that did not exist until you wrote it, because + was never about numbers. It was always about __add__. This is the whole of Chapter 19 in miniature.

3.9Truthiness

One more protocol deserves its own section, because it shapes how you write conditions throughout the language. Every object in Python can be tested for truth, in an if or a while or with the and, or, and not operators, not only the values True and False. When asked, an object reports its own truthiness through the __bool__ method, or failing that, through __len__ (an object with length zero is false). The values that count as false are few and worth memorising.

Falsy (count as False)Truthy (count as True)
False, Noneeverything not in the falsy column
0, 0.0, 0j, Decimal(0)any non-zero number
"" (empty string)any non-empty string

The idiomatic consequence is that you test a container's emptiness directly, rather than comparing its length to zero. Write if not names: rather than if len(names) == 0:; the first is shorter, reads more naturally, and works for any object that defines truthiness. This habit, small in itself, is one of the clearest marks of code written by someone who understands the object model rather than merely uses it.

    def __bool__(self):

Solution 3.1: Everything has a type

type([]) is <class 'list'>, and type(list) is <class 'type'>, so type(type([])) is <class 'type'>. The answer is type because every type is itself an object whose type is type, the object responsible for creating types. The chain stops here because type is its own type.

Solution 3.2: Predict the aliasing

a prints [1, 2, 3, 4], b prints [1, 2, 3, 4], and c prints [1, 2, 3, 5]. Because b = a binds b to the same object as a, appending through b is visible through a (they are one list). But c = a[:] makes an independent copy, so appending 5 to c affects only c. The lesson: = shares, slicing copies.

Solution 3.3: Immutable rebinding

x = 10
print(id(x)); x += 1; print(id(x))   # two DIFFERENT ids

lst = [1]
print(id(lst)); lst.append(2); print(id(lst))   # SAME id

An int is immutable, so x += 1 cannot change the existing integer; it computes a new integer and rebinds x to it, giving a new id. A list is mutable, so append changes the existing object in place, and its id is unchanged.

Solution 3.4: The function-call puzzle

def mutate(lst):
    lst.append(99)        # changes the shared object

def rebind(lst):
    lst = [99]            # binds the LOCAL name to a new object

data = [1, 2]
mutate(data); print(data)   # [1, 2, 99]
rebind(data); print(data)   # [1, 2, 99], rebind did nothing visible

mutate changes the one object both names point at, so the caller sees it. rebind only re-points its local parameter at a new list; the caller's name still points at the original, so nothing changes for the caller. The parameter is just another name bound by the call.

Solution 3.5: is versus ==

Two distinct lists with equal contents ([1] == [1] is True, [1] is [1] is False) satisfy the first case; any object compared with itself, or None is None, satisfies the second. The integers 256 are the same object because CPython caches every small integer from -5 to 256 and reuses it, so is is True. The integer 257 is outside that cache, so each occurrence is a fresh object and is is False. In both cases == is True, which is why you must use == for value comparison.

Solution 3.6: Why can't a list be a key?

Using a list as a key raises TypeError: unhashable type: 'list'. A dictionary stores each entry in a location derived from its key's hash. If the key were mutable, you could change it after insertion, its hash would change, and the dictionary would look in the wrong place and fail to find the entry. Forbidding mutable keys prevents this corruption. To represent the same ordered data as a key, use a tuple, which is immutable and therefore hashable: d[(1, 2)] = ....

Solution 3.7: Implement a dunder

class Vector:
    def __init__(self, x, y):
        self.x, self.y = x, y
    def __eq__(self, other):
        return (self.x, self.y) == (other.x, other.y)

With __eq__ defined, Vector(1, 2) == Vector(1, 2) is True. But adding the vectors to a set raises TypeError: unhashable type: 'Vector'. This is by design: when you define __eq__, Python sets __hash__ to None, because the default hash (based on identity) would no longer agree with your new equality. To make the vectors usable in a set or as keys, add a matching __hash__, for example def __hash__(self): return hash((self.x, self.y)), honouring the contract that equal objects share a hash.

Solution 3.8: Truthiness

False: 0, 0.0, "", [], None, {}. True: " " (a space is a non-empty string), [0] (a non-empty list, even though its only element is falsy), {0: 0} (a non-empty dict). The one that most often surprises beginners is [0]: a list containing a single zero is truthy, because truthiness of a container depends on whether it is empty, not on the truthiness of its contents. Likewise " " catches people who forget a space is a real character.

Numbers in Five Forms

Most languages give you two kinds of number and leave you to suffer their limitations. Python gives you five, each a deliberate trade between range, exactness, and speed. Choosing the right one is a real skill, and choosing the wrong one has, more than once, destroyed rockets and bankrupted rounding errors into fortunes.

The fast drives out the slow even if the fast is wrong. — William Kahan, principal architect of the IEEE 754 floating-point standard

Numbers feel like the simplest thing a computer could possibly handle, and that feeling is a trap. We learned numbers as children and stopped questioning them, but the machine does not have the luxury of the infinite paper on which we were taught to do long division. Every numeric type a computer offers is a compromise: how large a value may grow, how precisely it may be held, how quickly it may be calculated. A language's choice of numeric types is therefore a choice about which compromises to expose and which to hide, and Python's choice is unusually generous. Where C offers a handful of fixed-size integers and two sizes of float, Python offers five distinct numeric types, and understanding when each is the right tool is part of what separates a fluent programmer from a fluent typist.

This chapter covers all five: the unbounded integer, the floating-point real, the exact decimal, the exact rational, and the complex number. It explains how each is stored, where each is exact and where each lies, and how Python decides what happens when you mix them. Everything here is a direct application of Chapter 3: numbers are objects, arithmetic is method dispatch, and the differences between the types are differences in what those objects promise.

4.1The Numeric Tower

Before the individual types, the structure that relates them. Python organises numbers into an abstract hierarchy, defined formally in the numbers module and known informally as the numeric tower. Each level is a more specialised kind of number than the one above it, and each concrete type you actually use slots in at the appropriate level.

The tower exists so that code can reason about numbers in general without caring about their exact type. A function that needs any whole number can test isinstance(n, numbers.Integral) and accept both int and bool; a function that needs any real value can test against numbers.Real. You will not use the numbers module often, but knowing the tower is there explains why int and float cooperate so smoothly, and why Decimal sometimes refuses to.

4.2Integers: Unbounded by Design

Python's int has no maximum value. It grows as large as the memory of your machine allows, and arithmetic on it never overflows. This is unlike C, Java, and most other languages, where an integer is a fixed-size box (commonly 32 or 64 bits) that silently wraps around when it overflows, the flaw that, as Chapter 1 described, destroyed the Ariane 5 rocket. Python pays for safety with speed: a Python integer is a full object, not a raw machine word, and arithmetic on it is slower. For the vast majority of programs the safety is worth far more than the speed.

4.3Floats and the IEEE 754 Reality

Python's float is a 64-bit IEEE 754 double-precision number, the exact layout we dissected in Figure 1.11: one sign bit, eleven exponent bits, fifty-two fraction bits. It is the same floating-point type used by C, Java, JavaScript, and essentially every modern processor, and it carries the same famous hazard. A float is an approximation of a real number, not an exact value, and the approximation occasionally surprises you.

0.1 + 0.2            # 0.30000000000000004
0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3     # False

This is not a bug in Python; it is the nature of representing decimal fractions in binary, and Chapter 1 explained why 0.1 has no exact binary form. What is worth adding here is why the interpreter usually hides the problem. Since Python 3.1, the repr of a float prints the shortest decimal string that round-trips back to the same underlying value, so repr(0.1) shows the tidy 0.1 even though the stored value is the long expansion. The display is honest about the value's identity but merciful about its ugliness. The ugliness only surfaces when arithmetic makes two approximations disagree, as in 0.1 + 0.2.

The correct way to compare two floats is therefore not == but a tolerance check. The math module provides one built for the purpose, math.isclose, which compares with both a relative and an absolute tolerance.

Example 4.2Comparing floats safely, and the special values
import math
math.isclose(0.1 + 0.2, 0.3)        # True

# IEEE 754 also defines three values that are not ordinary numbers:
math.inf                              # positive infinity
-math.inf                             # negative infinity
math.nan                              # "not a number", the result of 0/0, inf-inf, etc.

# nan is the one value not equal to itself:
math.nan == math.nan                  # False!
math.isnan(math.nan)                  # True, the correct way to test

import sys
sys.float_info.max                    # 1.7976931348623157e+308
sys.float_info.epsilon                # 2.220446049250313e-16

The behaviour of nan is not a Python quirk; the IEEE standard mandates that nan compare unequal to everything, including itself. Code that sorts or deduplicates floating-point data must handle it deliberately, or it will behave strangely in ways that are maddening to debug.

History

In 1994, a flaw in the floating-point division unit of Intel's new Pentium processor produced wrong answers for certain divisions. Intel initially dismissed it as rare, then, under public pressure, recalled the chips at a cost of around 475 million dollars. The episode established the modern expectation that arithmetic hardware must be provably correct, and it remains the textbook example of why floating-point behaviour is treated with such seriousness.

4.4Decimal: When the Cents Must Add Up

When a bank totals an account or a tax authority computes what is owed, floating-point error is not a curiosity but a liability. For these uses Python provides decimal.Decimal, a type that performs arithmetic in base ten, exactly as a careful human would with pencil and paper, so that 0.1 + 0.2 is precisely 0.3 and not a hair more.

Example 4.3Decimal arithmetic, and the construction trap
from decimal import Decimal, getcontext, ROUND_HALF_UP

Decimal("0.1") + Decimal("0.2")        # Decimal('0.3'), exact

# THE TRAP: build Decimals from strings, never from floats.
Decimal(0.1)      # Decimal('0.1000000000000000055511151231257...')
Decimal("0.1")    # Decimal('0.1'), clean

# A currency calculation, rounded to cents:
price = Decimal("19.99")
tax   = Decimal("0.0825")
total = (price * (1 + tax)).quantize(Decimal("0.01"), rounding=ROUND_HALF_UP)
print(total)        # 21.64

The trap is worth stating loudly: Decimal(0.1) captures the float's error, because by the time the literal 0.1 reaches the Decimal constructor it is already a flawed binary float. Decimal("0.1"), from a string, is exact. Always build money values from strings.

A Decimal carries a context that controls how many significant digits it keeps (the default is 28) and how it rounds (the default is banker's rounding, which we meet again in Section 4.9). You can adjust the context globally with getcontext() when a calculation needs more or fewer digits, and quantize rounds a value to a fixed number of decimal places, which is exactly what currency requires.

4.5Fraction: Exact Rationals

Where Decimal is exact in base ten, fractions.Fraction is exact in every base, because it stores a number as a true ratio of two integers, a numerator and a denominator, always reduced to lowest terms. One third plus one sixth is exactly one half, with no rounding anywhere, because the arithmetic is done on the integers themselves.

from fractions import Fraction

Fraction(1, 3) + Fraction(1, 6)     # Fraction(1, 2), exact
Fraction("0.25") * 4                 # Fraction(1, 1)
Fraction(6, 8)                       # Fraction(3, 4), auto-reduced

# Recover a clean fraction from a messy float:
Fraction(0.5)                        # Fraction(1, 2)
Fraction(0.1).limit_denominator(1000)  # Fraction(1, 10), the intended value

Fractions are the right tool when exactness matters and the values are genuinely rational: probability calculations, symbolic mathematics, and anywhere a result like "two thirds" must stay two thirds rather than becoming 0.6666666666666666. The limit_denominator method is a small gem, recovering the simple fraction a human most likely intended from a float that approximates it.

4.6Complex Numbers

Python is one of the few mainstream languages with complex numbers built into the core, no import required. A complex literal is written with a j suffix on its imaginary part, following the engineering convention rather than the mathematician's i. Complex numbers are indispensable in signal processing, electrical engineering, and physics, and having them in the language proper rather than in a library makes that work noticeably cleaner.

z = 2 + 3j
z.real            # 2.0
z.imag            # 3.0
abs(z)            # 3.605551275463989, the magnitude (hypotenuse)
z.conjugate()     # (2-3j)

import cmath
cmath.sqrt(-1)      # 1j, the square root math.sqrt cannot give you
cmath.phase(z)    # 0.982..., the angle in radians

The companion module cmath provides the complex versions of the trigonometric and exponential functions that math offers only for reals. Note that math.sqrt(-1) raises an error, while cmath.sqrt(-1) happily returns 1j: the two modules embody different promises about what a number is allowed to be.

4.7A Surprise: bool Is an int

Here is a fact that startles most newcomers and that is, once seen, a small delight. In Python, bool is not a separate type alongside int; it is a subclass of int. True is, for every arithmetic purpose, equal to 1, and False is equal to 0. This was done in 2003 for backward compatibility, when booleans were added to a language that had long used 1 and 0 in their place, and it has proven quietly useful ever since.

True == 1                 # True
False == 0                # True
isinstance(True, int)     # True
True + True              # 2

# The practical payoff: counting with sum()
votes = [True, False, True, True, False]
sum(votes)               # 3, counts the True values

scores = [82, 45, 91, 67, 30]
sum(s >= 60 for s in scores)  # 3, how many passed

That last idiom, summing a sequence of comparisons to count how many are true, is genuinely Pythonic and reads cleanly once you know that a boolean is a number. It is a direct consequence of the object model: True is an object whose type happens to be a subclass of int, so it inherits every numeric behaviour.

4.8Mixing Types: Operator Dispatch and Division

What happens when you add an int to a float? The answer applies the data model from Chapter 3. The expression 1 + 2.0 first asks the integer, (1).__add__(2.0). The integer does not know how to add a float to itself and returns the special value NotImplemented. Python then asks the float, from the other side, (2.0).__radd__(1), and the float, which does know how to absorb an integer, converts the 1 to 1.0 and returns 3.0. The result climbs the numeric tower to the more general type.

The two divisions, and the sign of the remainder

Python has two division operators, and the difference trips up nearly everyone once. True division, /, always produces a float, even when the operands divide evenly: 6 / 2 is 3.0, not 3. Floor division, //, rounds the result down toward negative infinity, and the modulo operator, %, returns a remainder whose sign follows the divisor, not the dividend. This last rule differs from C and surprises people, but it has the elegant property that a % n is always in the range from 0 up to n for a positive n, which is usually what you want.

7 / 2        # 3.5 , always a float
7 // 2       # 3   , floor division
-7 // 2      # -4  , rounds toward negative infinity, not toward zero
-7 % 2       # 1   , sign follows the divisor (positive here)
divmod(17, 5)  # (3, 2), quotient and remainder together

Solution 4.2: The 0.1 problem

0.1 + 0.1 + 0.1 == 0.3 is False, because each 0.1 is a tiny bit off from the true one-tenth, the three errors accumulate, and the sum lands just beside the (also slightly off) stored value of 0.3. The correct comparison is math.isclose(0.1 + 0.1 + 0.1, 0.3), which is True because it allows for a small tolerance rather than demanding bit-for-bit equality.

Solution 4.3: Money done right

Solution 4.6: The sign of the remainder

-7 // 2 is -4 (floor division rounds down, toward negative infinity, not toward zero). -7 % 2 is 1 (the remainder's sign follows the positive divisor). 7 // -2 is -4 (again rounding down). 7 % -2 is -1 (the divisor is negative, so the remainder is too). The invariant that ties them together is (a // b) * b + (a % b) == a, which holds in every case.

Sorting raises no error but produces a meaningless order, because the comparisons used by the sort all involve nan, which returns False for every ordering question, so nan lands wherever it happened to start. Strings & Text

Most real programs spend most of their time moving text around: reading it, cleaning it, searching it, assembling it into reports. Python's handling of text is among the finest in any language, and it rests on one decision that took the industry fifty years to get right: a string is a sequence of characters, not a sequence of bytes.

We were eating dinner in a New Jersey diner. Ken wrote it on the back of a placemat. By the time we got back to the office, UTF-8 was essentially done. — Rob Pike, recalling the 1992 design of the UTF-8 encoding with Ken Thompson

There is a quiet revolution buried in the difference between Python 2 and Python 3, and it is almost entirely about text. In the older language, a string was a sequence of bytes, and the unspoken assumption that one byte equalled one character worked beautifully for English and collapsed for everyone else. Python 3 made the harder, correct choice: a string is a sequence of characters, abstract symbols drawn from every writing system humanity has ever devised, and bytes are a separate thing you convert to and from deliberately. This single change broke a great deal of old code and saved a generation of new programmers from a category of bug so common it has its own name. Understanding it is the foundation on which everything else in this chapter rests.

We will start with what a string actually is, take a necessary detour through Unicode and encodings (the part most books skip and most programmers later wish they had not), then cover the practical craft: writing string literals, slicing them, the methods that do the daily work, the three generations of formatting, how to build strings without quadratic slowdown, the binary counterpart in bytes, and a first proper look at regular expressions.

5.1What a String Really Is

A Python string is an immutable sequence of Unicode code points. A code point is simply a number assigned to a character by the Unicode standard: the capital letter A is code point 65, the Greek alpha is 945, the Devanagari letter क is 2325, and the snake emoji is 128013. A string is a sequence of these numbers, and the characters you see on screen are how they are drawn. Crucially, one element of a Python string is one code point, regardless of how many bytes that code point will take when it is eventually stored or transmitted.

Two built-in functions convert between a character and its code point, and they are the cleanest way to see the principle in action.

ord("A")        # 65  , the code point of a character
chr(65)         # 'A' , the character for a code point
ord("🐍")       # 128013
chr(128013)     # '🐍'

len("🐍")       # 1   , ONE code point, even though it is an emoji

That final line is worth dwelling on. In Python 3, the length of the snake emoji is one, because it is one character. In JavaScript and Java, whose strings are sequences of sixteen-bit units, the same emoji has a length of two, because it does not fit in sixteen bits and must be stored as a pair. Python spares you that complication entirely: a character is a character, and its length is one.

Code Point

An integer between 0 and 1,114,111 (0x10FFFF) that the Unicode standard assigns to a single character, written in the form U+0041 for the letter A. A Python string is a sequence of code points. How those code points are turned into actual bytes for storage or transmission is a separate matter, called encoding, and is the subject of the next section.

5.2Unicode and Encodings

For the first decades of computing, the dominant character set was ASCII, which used seven bits to encode 128 symbols: the English alphabet in two cases, the digits, punctuation, and some control codes. It was an American standard for an American industry, and it had no room for the accented letters of French, the umlauts of German, the Cyrillic of Russian, or the thousands of characters of Chinese. A patchwork of incompatible eight-bit and multi-byte extensions grew up to fill the gap, and text written under one scheme turned to garbage when read under another. The garbage was so common that the Japanese gave it a name, mojibake, "character transformation".

Unicode, begun in 1987, was the fix: a single universal catalogue assigning a unique code point to every character in every script ever used by humans, with room for more than a million. But Unicode only assigns the numbers. It does not say how to store them as bytes, and that is the job of an encoding. The same code point can be turned into bytes in several ways, and the dominant way, by an overwhelming margin, is UTF-8.

History

UTF-8 was designed in September 1992 by Ken Thompson and Rob Pike, the creators of Unix and later Go, over dinner at a diner in New Jersey, with the first draft written on a placemat. Its genius is that the 128 ASCII characters keep their original single-byte values, so every ASCII file is already a valid UTF-8 file, while other characters expand to two, three, or four bytes. This backward compatibility is why UTF-8 conquered the web: adopting it cost nothing for existing English text.

Example 5.1Encoding, decoding, and how the bytes differ
s = "café 🐍"
len(s)                       # 6 characters

raw = s.encode("utf-8")        # turn text into bytes
type(raw)                    # <class 'bytes'>
len(raw)                     # 9 bytes: 'é' takes 2, '🐍' takes 4

raw.decode("utf-8")           # 'café 🐍', back to text

# Decoding with the WRONG encoding produces mojibake or an error:
raw.decode("latin-1")         # 'cafÃ© ð\x9f\x90\x8d', garbage

The last line is the bug that haunted text processing for decades: the bytes were correct, but they were interpreted under the wrong encoding, and the result is nonsense. The rule that prevents it is simple and absolute: always know the encoding of the bytes you are decoding, and when in doubt, assume UTF-8, because on the modern web it is almost always right.

5.3How CPython Stores Strings

If a string is a sequence of code points, and code points range up to a million, does every character cost four bytes of memory? For most strings that would be wasteful, since a string of pure English needs only one byte per character. CPython solves this elegantly with a scheme introduced in version 3.3 and described in PEP 393, the flexible string representation. The interpreter looks at the largest code point in a string and picks the narrowest storage width that can hold it: one byte per character if every code point fits in 255, two bytes if every code point fits in 65,535, and four bytes only if some character truly needs it.

This design buys two things at once. It keeps the common case, ASCII and Latin text, as compact as the old byte strings were, so upgrading to proper Unicode cost almost no memory. And because every character in a given string is the same width, the interpreter can jump straight to the i-th character by multiplying, which means indexing a string and asking its length are both instant, no matter how long the string or how exotic its characters. Languages that store text as UTF-8 internally cannot promise that; they must walk the string to find a character by position. Python's choice trades a little memory for guaranteed fast indexing, and for most programs it is the right trade.

5.4Writing String Literals

Python offers several ways to write a string in source code, each suited to a different need. The choice among them is mostly about avoiding the need to escape characters, which keeps text readable.

a = 'single quotes'

c = "she said \"hi\""           # escape the inner quotes
d = 'she said "hi"'             # cleaner: use the other quote

e = """A triple-quoted string
spans multiple lines

f = r"C:\Users\Ada\new"          # raw: backslashes are literal, not escapes

g = ("Python automatically joins "   # adjacent string literals
     "two literals with no operator "  # become one string
     "between them.")

The raw string, prefixed with r, deserves emphasis because it prevents a common class of error. Inside an ordinary string, a backslash begins an escape sequence: \n is a newline, \t a tab, \\ a single backslash. This is a problem for Windows file paths and for regular expressions, both of which are full of backslashes. A raw string turns the escaping off, so r"\n" is a backslash followed by an n, two characters, not one newline. You will use raw strings constantly once you reach regular expressions in Section 5.10.

5.5Indexing, Slicing, and Immutability

A string is a sequence, so it supports the same indexing and slicing as any sequence, which we will meet again with lists in Chapter 8. Each character has an index from zero, negative indices count from the end, and a slice extracts a range using the notation [start:stop:step], where stop is exclusive.

s = "Pythonic"
s[0]        # 'P'
s[-1]       # 'c', last character
s[0:6]      # 'Python', indices 0 through 5
s[:6]       # 'Python', start defaults to 0
s[6:]       # 'ic'    , stop defaults to the end
s[::2]      # 'Pton'  , every second character
s[::-1]     # 'cinohtyP', reversed

The fact you must never forget is that strings are immutable, exactly as Chapter 3 described. You cannot change a character in place; an attempt raises a TypeError. Every operation that appears to modify a string in fact creates a new one. This is not a limitation to resent but a guarantee to rely on: because a string can never change underneath you, you can pass it to any function, use it as a dictionary key, and share it freely, knowing no one can alter it. The one place immutability bites is when you build a long string piece by piece in a loop, and Section 5.8 shows the right way to do that.

5.7Formatting: Three Generations

Assembling a string from values, a name, a number, a date, is so common that Python has accumulated three ways to do it over its history. You should write all new code in the third, but you will read the other two in older code and must recognise them.

name, score = "Ada", 91.5

# 1. Percent formatting (oldest, inherited from C). Still seen in logging.
"%s scored %.1f" % (name, score)        # 'Ada scored 91.5'

# 2. str.format (Python 2.6+). Verbose but still common.
"{} scored {:.1f}".format(name, score)   # 'Ada scored 91.5'

# 3. f-strings (Python 3.6+). The modern default. Use these.
f"{name} scored {score:.1f}"             # 'Ada scored 91.5'

The f-string, marked by an f before the opening quote, is the clearest of the three because the values appear inline, exactly where they will be in the output. Anything inside the braces is a full Python expression, evaluated on the spot, and after a colon you may add a format specification controlling width, alignment, precision, sign, and grouping. The specification is its own compact mini-language, and the most useful pieces of it fit in one example.

Example 5.3The f-string format specification
n = 1234567
pi = 3.14159265
name = "Ada"

f"{pi:.2f}"         # '3.14'      , two decimal places
f"{n:,}"            # '1,234,567' , thousands separators
f"{n:_}"            # '1_234_567' , underscore grouping
f"{0.875:.1%}"      # '87.5%'     , as a percentage
f"{255:#x}"         # '0xff'      , hexadecimal with prefix
f"{name:>10}"       # '       Ada', right-align in width 10
f"{name:^10}"       # '   Ada    ', centre
f"{name:*<10}"      # 'Ada*******', left-align, pad with *

# The self-documenting form (Python 3.8+), invaluable for debugging:
f"{score=}"         # 'score=91.5', prints the name and the value

The self-documenting form, an equals sign just before the closing brace, prints both the expression and its value. It is the fastest way to scatter informative debugging output through a program, and it is worth committing to memory now.

5.8Building Strings Without Quadratic Slowdown

Because strings are immutable, a deceptively innocent loop can become catastrophically slow, and this is one of the few performance traps worth learning before you ever hit it. Consider building one long string by repeatedly adding to it with +=. Each += cannot change the existing string, since strings are immutable, so it must create a new string by copying every character built so far and then appending the next piece. Building a string of n pieces this way copies roughly n-squared characters in total, which crawls to a halt as n grows.

The idiomatic remedy is to collect the pieces in a list, which is mutable and appends cheaply, and to join them in a single operation at the end. The join method computes the total length once, allocates the result once, and copies each piece once, turning quadratic work into linear.

# Slow: O(n²), re-copies on every iteration
result = ""
for piece in pieces:
    result += piece

# Fast and idiomatic: O(n)
result = "".join(pieces)

# When the pieces are produced incrementally, io.StringIO is the tool:
import io
buffer = io.StringIO()
for piece in pieces:
    buffer.write(piece)
result = buffer.getvalue()
An honest caveat

CPython contains a special optimisation that sometimes makes s += piece run in linear time when s has no other references. It is real but fragile: it does not apply on other implementations like PyPy, and it breaks the moment a second name points at the string. Do not rely on it. The join idiom is always fast and always clear, so simply make it a habit.

5.9Bytes and Bytearray

Text is what your program reasons about; bytes are what crosses its boundaries. When you read a file, receive a network message, compute a cryptographic hash, or talk to hardware, you are handling raw bytes, and Python gives you the bytes type for exactly this. A bytes object is an immutable sequence of integers from 0 to 255, written with a b prefix, and its mutable sibling is bytearray.

data = b"hello"
type(data)            # <class 'bytes'>
data[0]              # 104, an integer, not a character
data.decode("utf-8")   # 'hello', turn bytes back into text

# Hashing operates on bytes, not text, so you must encode first:
import hashlib
hashlib.sha256("password".encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
# '5e884898da28047151d0e56f8dc6292773603d0d6aabbdd62a11ef721d1542d8'

The discipline that keeps text handling sane is to convert at the boundaries and reason in text within. Decode bytes to str as soon as they enter your program, do all your work on strings, and encode back to bytes only when you write out. This is sometimes called the "Unicode sandwich": bytes on the outside, text in the middle. Programs that mix the two carelessly are exactly the programs that produce mojibake.

5.10A First Look at Regular Expressions

When the text you are searching has a shape rather than a fixed form, a phone number, an email address, a date, a log line, plain string methods are not enough, and you reach for the re module and its regular expressions. A regular expression is a small, dense pattern language: \d matches any digit, \w any word character, . any character at all, while *, +, and ? control repetition. Patterns are almost always written as raw strings, because they are full of backslashes.

Example 5.4The four operations you will use most
import re
text = "Reach me at ada@example.com or bob@work.org by Friday."

# findall: every match, as a list
re.findall(r"[\w.]+@[\w.]+", text)
# ['ada@example.com', 'bob@work.org']

# search: the first match anywhere, or None
m = re.search(r"(\w+)@(\w+)", text)
m.group(0)        # 'ada@example', the whole match
m.group(1)        # 'ada'       , first parenthesised group

# sub: replace every match
re.sub(r"[\w.]+@[\w.]+", "[email hidden]", text)
# 'Reach me at [email hidden] or [email hidden] by Friday.'

# fullmatch: does the WHOLE string fit the pattern? (validation)
bool(re.fullmatch(r"\d{4}-\d{2}-\d{2}", "2026-05-27"))   # True
Use them sparingly

Regular expressions are powerful and easy to abuse. If you are searching for fixed text, use str methods like in, find, or replace, which are clearer and faster. If your data is structured, such as JSON, HTML, or CSV, use a real parser, never a regular expression, because those formats are not regular and a regex will eventually meet input it mishandles. Reach for regular expressions when the shape is genuinely regular but variable: identifiers, phone numbers, log formats. A famous saying warns: faced with a problem, some people think "I will use regular expressions", and now they have two problems.

Booleans & Truthiness

Two values, three operators, one protocol. The whole machinery of decision in Python rests on a remarkably small idea, formalised by a self-taught English schoolmaster in 1854 and quietly built into every modern computer ever since.

The respective interpretation of the symbols 0 and 1 in the system of logic are Nothing and Universe. — George Boole, An Investigation of the Laws of Thought, 1854

There is a beautiful symmetry in the fact that the language of computation, born of vacuum tubes and silicon, rests on a mathematics that was published a hundred and fifty years before any computer ran. Before Babbage's Engine, before electronic switching, before the very word algorithm meant what we mean by it today, George Boole worked out, in a small house in Cork, the algebra by which true and false could be manipulated like numbers. Two states, three operations, a handful of laws. That was enough, as it turned out, to organise every decision a computer would ever make.

This chapter is short because the topic is small, but the topic is small in the way the alphabet is small: the rest of the language sits on top of it. We will look at Python's two boolean values and how they behave; the comparison operators that produce them; the logical operators that combine them, with a wrinkle no other chapter has prepared you for; the rule of truthiness that decides whether any value, not just a boolean, counts as true; the one-line conditional expression; the recent match statement that brings structural pattern matching to Python; and De Morgan's laws, which let you rewrite tangled negations into readable code.

6.1The Two Values

Python has exactly two boolean values: True and False. Both are written capitalised, both are global names that refer to single, shared objects (no matter how many times you write True in a program, you are pointing at the very same object), and both belong to the type bool.

Now a fact that we met briefly in Chapter 4 and that bears restating, because the rest of the chapter quietly depends on it: bool is a subclass of int. True equals 1 in every arithmetic context, False equals 0, and isinstance(True, int) is true. This is not a strange accident; it was decided deliberately when booleans were added to the language in 2003 by PEP 285, so that all the code that had been using 0 and 1 as truth values for the previous twelve years would keep working unchanged. The consequence is small but useful: you can sum a sequence of comparisons to count how many are true.

scores = [82, 45, 91, 67, 30, 99]
sum(s >= 60 for s in scores)   # 4, how many passed
bool

The type of the two values True and False. It is a subclass of int: every True behaves as 1 and every False as 0 in arithmetic. Both values are singletons, so True is True and False is False are always trivially true, but you almost never need is with booleans; comparing for value is the same as comparing for identity, and the value test reads better.

Booleans are mostly produced by comparing two values. Python has six basic comparison operators, and they are the obvious ones with the obvious meanings.

Chained comparisons

Python lets you write comparisons in chains, and the meaning is exactly the mathematical one: 1 <= x <= 10 reads as "x is between 1 and 10 inclusive". The language is doing something subtle for you here. Under the hood, that single expression is the logical AND of two comparisons, with x evaluated only once: (1 <= x) and (x <= 10). The short-circuit behaviour of and (Section 6.3) means the second comparison is skipped entirely if the first is false.

a = 2
1 < a > 0                 # True, (1 < a) and (a > 0)
1 < a == 2                # True, (1 < a) and (a == 2)

In C, 1 < x < 10 means something quite different: (1 < x) < 10, which reduces to comparing a 0-or-1 result against 10, and is silently wrong. Python's chains are the kind of small detail that makes the language pleasant to read.

6.3and, or, not: Two Wrinkles

The three logical operators in Python read as English and behave, for boolean inputs, exactly as you would expect: a and b is true when both are, a or b is true when at least one is, and not a flips the value. Two things make them interesting, and both are worth knowing well because they shape idiomatic Python.

Short-circuit evaluation

The first wrinkle is that and and or are short-circuit. For a and b, Python evaluates a first; if it is falsy, the whole expression cannot be true, so b is not evaluated at all. For a or b, Python evaluates a; if it is truthy, the whole expression must be true, so b is skipped. This is more than a performance trick. It lets you guard one expression with another: x is not None and len(x) > 0 never calls len when x is None, because the first half short-circuits.

They return one of the operands, not a boolean

The second wrinkle is more surprising, and once you see it, a whole shelf of Python idioms suddenly makes sense. a and b does not return True or False; it returns one of the actual operands. Specifically, and returns the first falsy value it finds, or the last value if all are truthy; or returns the first truthy value, or the last value if all are falsy.

0 or "fallback"      # 'fallback' , 0 is falsy, so or returns the second operand
"Ada" or "Anon"     # 'Ada'      , first truthy value short-circuits
"Ada" and "Lovelace"  # 'Lovelace' , both truthy, and returns the last
0 and "never reached"   # 0         , first falsy short-circuits

# The classic default-value idiom:
display = user_input or "Guest"    # falls back when user_input is empty

not is the exception: it always returns a true boolean, True or False, never an operand. This asymmetry is occasionally a small frustration but more often a relief, because not is the operator you tend to use when you actually want a boolean.

Example 6.2A peek at the bytecode

The short-circuit is not a runtime decision; the compiler emits a conditional jump that skips the second operand entirely.

import dis
def f(a, b): return a and b
dis.dis(f)
  1   RESUME            0
      LOAD_FAST         a
      COPY              1
      POP_JUMP_IF_FALSE to RETURN_VALUE
      POP_TOP
      LOAD_FAST         b
      RETURN_VALUE

Read it: load a, keep a copy on the stack, jump straight to RETURN_VALUE if it is falsy (skipping b entirely), otherwise drop the copy and load b in its place. The compiler is doing the short-circuit; the interpreter never sees a choice.

6.4Truthiness, Revisited

Chapter 3.9 introduced the idea that every Python value can stand in for a boolean. This chapter is the right place to look at it again, because the rule shapes most of the conditions you will write. When Python tests a value for truth, it calls __bool__() on the value; if that is not defined, it falls back to __len__(), treating zero length as false; if neither is defined, the value is truthy. The set of values that come out falsy is small and worth memorising:

  • False and None
  • 0, 0.0, 0j, Decimal(0), Fraction(0)
  • "" (the empty string)
  • [], (), {}, set(), frozenset() (empty containers)
  • range(0) and any other empty sequence

Everything else is truthy. This rule is what makes if names: a complete way to say "if the list is not empty", and why while line := file.readline(): works as the loop's natural termination. It is also where some of the most pernicious silent bugs live, because truthiness asks about emptiness, not meaning. The string "False" is a non-empty string, so it is truthy; a configuration switch read from a file as text needs an explicit comparison, not a truth test.

A silent bug

A program reads a setting as text and uses if user_setting: to decide whether it is on. The user writes False in the config. The string "False" is non-empty, so the branch runs and the setting is treated as on, the opposite of what was meant. Truth tests ask "is there anything here?", not "is this thing meaningful?". When you mean the second, compare explicitly: if user_setting.lower() == "true":.

Example 6.3A class that decides its own truth
class Cart:
    def __init__(self):
        self.items = []
    def __bool__(self):
        return len(self.items) > 0

cart = Cart()
if not cart:
    print("Cart is empty")    # this runs
cart.items.append("apple")
if cart:
    print("Cart has items")   # this runs
Cart is empty
Cart has items

By defining __bool__, a class participates in the same if grammar as the built-in containers. If we had defined __len__ instead, the effect would be identical, because Python falls back to length when no __bool__ exists.

6.5The Conditional Expression

Python has one in-line form of if, called the conditional expression, that picks between two values:

status = "adult" if age >= 18 else "minor"

The shape is X if C else Y. Python evaluates the condition C first; if true, the whole expression yields X, otherwise Y. Like and and or, only one of the two value-expressions is evaluated, so the unused side cannot raise an error. The order is sometimes surprising on first reading: the value comes before the condition, not after. This is deliberate; it reads more naturally for the common case of "this value, when this is so, otherwise this other one".

Use it sparingly. The form is a small joy when it expresses a binary choice on one line. Nest it, or stretch it across two lines, and it becomes harder to read than the ordinary if block it tried to replace.

Style

If the entire expression does not fit comfortably on one line, write the ordinary block instead. Two levels of nested conditional expressions (a if c1 else b if c2 else d) are legal but discouraged; the linear ordering does not match how people parse English, and the reader has to mentally re-bracket them.

6.6Structural Pattern Matching: match

Released with Python 3.10 in October 2021, the match statement is the language's newest piece of control flow, and one of its most powerful. Pattern matching is not the same thing as a switch statement from C or Java, although a switch is roughly the simplest thing you can do with it. The full feature is closer to the destructuring of Haskell, Scala, or Rust: a match case is not just a value to compare against, it is a shape to compare against, and successfully matching a shape binds names to the parts.

The minimum example looks like a switch:

def classify(status):
    match status:
        case 200:
            return "OK"
        case 301 | 302:
            return "redirect"
        case 404:
            return "not found"
        case _:
            return "unknown"

The interesting part begins when the patterns describe shape:

Example 6.4Destructuring with match
def describe(point):
    match point:
        case (0, 0):
            return "origin"
        case (0, y):                       # captures the second component as y
            return f"on the y-axis at {y}"
        case (x, 0):
            return f"on the x-axis at {x}"
        case (x, y) if x == y:           # a guard: extra condition
            return f"diagonal at {x}"
        case (x, y):                       # anything else, with both components named
            return f"point ({x}, {y})"
        case _:
            return "not a point"

print(describe((0, 5)))   # on the y-axis at 5
print(describe((3, 3)))   # diagonal at 3
print(describe((7, 2)))   # point (7, 2)

Inside a case, a bare name (x, y) is a capture pattern: it matches anything and binds the matched value to that name. A literal (0) matches only equality. A guard, the if clause, adds an extra condition that the pattern alone cannot express. And the leading underscore is the wildcard, matching anything without binding a name.

The same patterns work for lists, dictionaries, and instances of your own classes. A dictionary pattern {"name": n, "age": a} succeeds when the subject is a mapping with at least those keys, binding their values. A class pattern Point(x=0, y=y) succeeds when the subject is a Point instance with x equal to zero, binding the y attribute. The feature is large enough that PEP 636, the official tutorial, is the best place to learn it properly. Used well, it replaces long chains of isinstance tests with code that reads as the data does.

One trap

A name that is just a bare lowercase identifier always means capture, not compare. To match against the value of a constant, the constant has to be dotted: case Color.RED:, not case RED:, the latter would simply rebind RED to whatever the subject was. The rule is documented in PEP 634 and surprises everyone exactly once.

6.7De Morgan's Laws and the Readable Negative

The last topic in this short chapter is not a feature of Python but a fact of logic, valuable because it lets you rewrite cluttered conditions into clean ones. Augustus De Morgan, who corresponded with Boole and shared his interests, observed in the 1840s that negation distributes over and and or by swapping them. In Python's notation:

not (a and b)   # is the same as   (not a) or  (not b)
not (a or  b)   # is the same as   (not a) and (not b)

The rule rarely matters in arithmetic; it matters in code, because negated conditions are harder to read than positive ones, and a needlessly negated combination of conditions is harder still. When you find yourself writing if not (x > 5 and x < 10):, De Morgan tells you it is the same as if x <= 5 or x >= 10:, which says what it means.

Example 6.5A condition rewritten three times
# Original, awkward: a double negative
if not (user.is_admin or user.has_token):
    refuse()

# De Morgan: distribute the not over the or, flipping it to and
if not user.is_admin and not user.has_token:
    refuse()

# Better still: flip the test and the action together
if user.is_admin or user.has_token:
    allow()
else:
    refuse()

All three forms are logically identical. The third reads in the positive, with the negation pushed onto a single keyword (else) rather than smeared across several. This is the kind of rewriting that earns its place in code review.

None & the Sentinel

A whole chapter for one value, because that one value carries surprising weight: it stands for absence, it powers the most common default-argument idiom, it is the source of more silent bugs than any other single feature, and its history reaches back to a billion-dollar regret.

I call it my billion-dollar mistake. It was the invention of the null reference in 1965. At that time, I was designing the first comprehensive type system for references in an object-oriented language. My goal was to ensure that all use of references should be absolutely safe, with checking performed automatically by the compiler. But I could not resist the temptation to put in a null reference, simply because it was so easy to implement. — Tony Hoare, QCon London 2009

Every language that lets you say "there is no value here" has to decide how. Some pick a special pointer that the programmer must everywhere remember to check, and pay the cost in null-pointer crashes for decades after. Some refuse the idea entirely and force you to wrap every absent thing in an explicit container. Python takes a middle path: it has a single, well-known object called None that stands for the absence of a value, hands it to you wherever a function has nothing useful to return, and gives you a small set of idioms for handling it well. Used correctly, it is one of the most pleasant null-equivalents in any language. Used carelessly, it produces exactly the bug class Hoare named for, with a softer landing.

This is a short chapter on a small subject, but the subject is load-bearing. Almost every function you write will need to think about None at least once: as a default argument, as a result for "nothing found", as a sentinel that distinguishes "no value given" from "the value given was None". We will cover all three, plus the type-hint conventions that pin None down statically, and a quick look at the family of "almost-None" values (nan, inf, empty strings, empty lists) that beginners sometimes conflate.

7.1The Idea of Absence

The number zero is not the absence of a number; it is a number with the value zero. The empty string is not the absence of text; it is a string with no characters. To express the genuine absence of a value, distinct from any specific value, you need a separate thing, and Python's separate thing is called None. It is what a function returns when it has nothing meaningful to return. It is what a database query gives back when there was no matching row. It is what a default-valued parameter holds when the caller did not supply one.

Why such an idea needs to exist at all is clearer after a moment with a counter-example. Suppose a function looks up a user by id and you decide that the "not found" answer should be the number zero. That works until someone valid is assigned id zero. Then you cannot tell whether the user is missing or simply has id zero, and the system has to be patched. The whole point of None is to be reliably outside every other value's domain: no real number is None, no real string is None, no real user is None. The price of this clarity is small but real: you must remember to check for None before treating a result as if it were the type you expected.

It is falsy, hashable, and immutable, which makes it a fine default value and a fine dictionary key. It has no methods of interest beyond what every object has.

7.2NoneType: One Type, One Value

None is unusual among Python's built-in values for being a true singleton: its type, NoneType, has exactly one instance, and the interpreter guarantees you will never see another. Attempting to create one fails by design, and the type cannot be subclassed.

type(None)            # <class 'NoneType'>
NoneType = type(None)
NoneType()              # returns None, the same None as before
NoneType() is None     # True, there is no second instance

id(None) == id(None)   # True, every reference points at the one object

This uniqueness is what makes the is None check (Section 7.3) both fast and reliable. There is also a quirk worth knowing about how None behaves with the rest of the language. It is falsy, so bool(None) is False and None in an if condition takes the false branch. But it cannot be ordered against anything: comparing None with < or > to a number raises TypeError, a change Python 3 made deliberately, because comparing apples to absence had no sensible answer in Python 2 either, and the silent "is less than everything" rule it used was a frequent source of bugs.

bool(None)        # False, None is falsy
None == 0          # False, None equals nothing but itself
None == None       # True
None < 5           # TypeError: '<' not supported between 'NoneType' and 'int'
A REPL quirk

When you type an expression into the REPL, the interpreter prints the value's repr. The one exception is when the value is None: the REPL hides it, to keep the screen quiet for the many functions that return None on purpose. So print("hi") shows you only the printed output, not also a separate None line; the None that print actually returned is silently swallowed. This is helpful but occasionally confusing: some calls that look like they returned nothing did return None.

7.3The is None Idiom

To check whether a value is None, write x is None, never x == None. The recommendation appears in PEP 8 and is one of the few style rules with a real technical reason behind it.

Three reasons matter. First, speed: is compares object identities, which in CPython is a single pointer comparison; == calls __eq__, which is a method dispatch. The difference is microscopic per call but real. Second, correctness: a perverse class could define __eq__ to return True when compared with None, so x == None might lie. Nothing can fake identity with the one true None; is None always gives the right answer. Third, style: the rest of the Python ecosystem uses is None, and consistency is its own value.

Example 7.1Two writes of the same check
# Idiomatic
if result is None:
    handle_missing()

# Works, but slower and stylistically wrong
if result == None:
    handle_missing()

# Negated form: always 'is not None', not 'not ... is None'
if result is not None:
    use(result)

The phrasing x is not None reads as English and is the canonical positive form. Avoid not x is None, which is parsed correctly (not (x is None)) but reads ambiguously to a human.

A frequent mistake

Beginners sometimes write if x: intending to check whether x is None. This works some of the time, because None is falsy, but it also treats 0, "", [], and every other falsy value as if they were missing. A function whose valid result might legitimately be 0 must distinguish "got nothing" from "got zero" by writing if x is None: explicitly.

7.4None as the Default Sentinel

The single most useful place for None in everyday Python is the default-argument idiom. We met the underlying trap in Chapter 3 and again briefly when introducing functions: a mutable default value (def f(items=[])) is created once and shared across every call, with the surprising consequence that the "default" accumulates state across calls. The remedy is to use None as the default and build the real default inside the function.

Example 7.2The mutable-default fix, in three steps
# Wrong: the empty list is created ONCE, when the function is defined.
def append_item(item, items=[]):
    items.append(item)
    return items

append_item(1)        # [1]
append_item(2)        # [1, 2], the default REMEMBERED the previous call!

# Right: None signals "no list given", then build a fresh one each call.
def append_item(item, items=None):
    if items is None:
        items = []
    items.append(item)
    return items

append_item(1)        # [1]
append_item(2)        # [2], fresh empty list each time

This pattern is so common that you should consider it a reflex: whenever a default value would naturally be a mutable object (a list, a dict, a set, an instance of your own class), the default goes into the signature as None and the real value is constructed in the body.

None is the right sentinel here for several reasons. It is unique, so the test if x is None cannot be confused with a real value. It is immutable, so it cannot accumulate state. It is well-known, so a reader of the function knows immediately what is going on. And it has no methods worth calling by accident.

7.5When None Is a Valid Value: Custom Sentinels

The default-argument idiom of Section 7.4 works perfectly until the function is one that legitimately accepts None as a value. Imagine a configuration getter that should distinguish three cases: "the caller gave us no default" (return whatever is stored, even None), "the caller passed a default of None" (return None when missing), and "the caller passed a real default" (return that). The first and second cases look identical to if x is None, because in the first case the parameter is also None. We need a sentinel that is not None.

The standard pattern is to create a unique private object that no caller could ever pass by accident.

Example 7.3A custom sentinel for "argument not provided"
_MISSING = object()   # a plain object, unique by identity

def get_config(key, default=_MISSING):
    if key in config_store:
        return config_store[key]
    if default is _MISSING:
        raise KeyError(key)         # caller gave no default → fail loudly
    return default                # caller's default, which may be None

get_config("theme")              # KeyError if missing
get_config("theme", default=None)  # None if missing, but distinct from "not given"

An empty object() instance is the simplest sentinel you can make. Each call to object() produces a fresh instance with its own identity, and no caller, anywhere, can ever pass it by accident, because they have no name for it. The is _MISSING check then cleanly separates the two cases.

This pattern appears throughout the standard library. The dict.pop method takes an optional default with exactly this design: when no default is supplied, popping a missing key raises KeyError; when a default is supplied (which may be None), the method returns it. The dataclasses module exports a MISSING sentinel for the same purpose. And argparse uses a SUPPRESS sentinel to mean "do not even set this attribute".

7.6None in Type Hints: Optional and T | None

When you annotate a function's signature, declaring that a parameter or return value can be None is part of the type. The typing module gives you two equivalent ways to say it.

from typing import Optional

# Older style (Python 3.5+):
def find_user(name: str) -> Optional[User]:
    ...

# Modern style (Python 3.10+, PEP 604):
def find_user(name: str) -> User | None:
    ...

Optional[T] means exactly Union[T, None], which since Python 3.10 can be written as T | None. The two are interchangeable; T | None is the modern preference because it does not need an import. Use the annotation wherever a function might legitimately return None (a lookup that may not find anything), or wherever an argument's default is None. A type checker like mypy or pyright will then refuse to let you call a method on the result without first checking, which is exactly the discipline that converts Hoare's billion-dollar mistake into a compiler-level annoyance.

def find_user(name: str) -> User | None:
    ...

u = find_user("Ada")
u.greet()                # mypy error: u may be None
if u is not None:
    u.greet()            # OK: inside this branch mypy narrows the type to User

Several other values look superficially like absence and are sometimes used as if they meant the same thing. They do not, and conflating them is the source of small bugs that can be very hard to track down. A short reference table:

Two pairs deserve a closer look. None and nan are both used for "I do not have a usable answer", but their semantics differ: None is the universal absence, while nan is specifically the floating-point sentinel for an undefined numeric result, and it propagates through arithmetic (any operation involving nan returns nan). Example 7.4nan is unequal to itself
import math
math.nan == math.nan       # False, yes, really
math.nan is math.nan       # True, but only because there is one nan object here
math.isnan(math.nan)       # True, the correct way to test for nan

None == None              # True, None behaves the way you expect

nan's refusal to equal itself is mandated by IEEE 754 and is not Python's fault; every modern language inherits the rule. The practical consequence is that you cannot deduplicate or sort a list containing nan reliably, and a list that might hold both real numbers and absences is usually better expressed with None than with nan.

Solution 7.2: is None versus == None

class Lying:
    def __eq__(self, other):

Solution 7.3: The mutable-default trap

# Bug demonstration:

The mutable list in the original signature is created exactly once, when def runs, and shared across every call. Substituting None as the signal-only default forces each call to build a fresh list.

    if key in d:

    return default

_MISSING is a fresh, private object whose identity no caller can ever match by accident. The is _MISSING test separates "no default passed" from "default passed and it happens to be None", which the plain default is None test cannot do.

Solution 7.5: Type-narrowed access

x = first(xs)

    print(x)

The annotated return type is int | None, so the static type of first(xs) at the call site is the union. Calling .bit_length() directly would fail under a type checker because None has no bit_length method. Solution 7.6: None versus nan

import math
xs = [1.0, None, 2.0, float("nan"), 3.0]

[x for x in xs if x is not None]
# [1.0, 2.0, nan, 3.0], nan is not None, so it passed through

# (b) Correct filter, drops both:
[x for x in xs if x is not None and not math.isnan(x)]

nan is a perfectly real float object; only its value is special. The is not None check has nothing to grab onto with nan, so it lets it through, and the explicit math.isnan test is required. Solution 7.8: A small library question

dict.get(key) returns None when the key is missing; dict.get(key, default) returns default instead. 8.1The Workhorse Container

A list in Python is an ordered, mutable sequence of values. It is written in square brackets, its elements may be of any type (even mixed), and its length may change at any moment by appending, inserting, or removing items. That sentence describes the surface; the rest of the chapter unpacks the consequences.

fruits   = ["apple", "banana", "cherry"]      # homogeneous; the common case
mixed    = [42, "hello", True, None, [1, 2]]    # mixed; legal, occasionally useful
empty    = []                                       # a list of nothing
numbers  = list(range(5))                          # [0, 1, 2, 3, 4], from any iterable

The choice to make one list type the default is a Python design decision worth pausing on. In Python, lists are ordered, growable arrays: random access by index is fast (constant time), appending at the end is fast (amortised constant), and almost everything you would actually want to do with a sequence is supported. The price you pay is that operations near the front of the list are slow, because the array has to shift; for those, you reach for collections.deque, which we will meet in Chapter 12. But the alternative cost is sharper: forcing every beginner to learn three sequence types and choose between them, before they have written a useful program, would have been a worse trade for the language. Python's principle here is the Zen line at the head of the chapter.

List

An ordered, mutable sequence written as [v1, v2, ...]. The type is list. Implemented in CPython as a dynamic array of PyObject * pointers, which makes random access and append fast (O(1)) and operations near the front slow (O(n)). It is the default container in Python for any "collection of things".

8.2How a List Is Stored

Almost every performance fact about Python lists falls out of one structural decision: a list is implemented in CPython as a resizable array of pointers to objects. It is not a linked list (despite the name) and never has been. Each slot in the array holds a PyObject *, an address pointing at an object that lives elsewhere in memory. The list itself owns the array; the objects it points at are owned by the reference-counting system we met in Chapter 2.

Two practical consequences follow at once. Random access is fast, because reaching lst[k] requires only adding k times a pointer-size to a known base address: constant time, regardless of the list's length. Append is amortised fast, because Python over-allocates the underlying array; pushing one item usually just writes into a pre-reserved slot. When the reserved slots run out, Python grows the array geometrically (roughly 12.5% over the current size, rounded to round numbers), copying once. The cost of that copy spreads across all the appends that filled the array, so on average each append is constant time.

The flip side is also forced: inserting at or near the front is O(n), because every later slot has to shift up by one to make room. Deleting from the middle is O(n) for the same reason. And searching for membership (x in lst) is O(n) because the list has no idea where to look; if you need fast membership, you need a set or a dict instead.

8.3Creating and Indexing

Lists are created in three forms. Most often, you write a list literal with square brackets. When the items already live in another iterable (a string, a range, a generator), you call list() to materialise them. When you need a fixed-size list initialised with a single value, you multiply.

letters = ["a", "b", "c"]            # literal
chars   = list("hello")             # ['h', 'e', 'l', 'l', 'o']
zeros   = [0] * 5                       # [0, 0, 0, 0, 0]
merged  = [*[1, 2], *[3, 4]]         # [1, 2, 3, 4], splat-merge

Once a list exists, you reach into it the same way you reached into a string in Chapter 5. The mechanics are identical (Python's sequence protocol is uniform), so we cover them quickly here.

xs = [10, 20, 30, 40, 50]
xs[0]            # 10                first
xs[-1]           # 50                last (negative wraps)
xs[1:4]          # [20, 30, 40]      slice: half-open [start, stop)
xs[:3]           # [10, 20, 30]      from the start
xs[3:]           # [40, 50]          to the end
xs[::2]          # [10, 30, 50]      every second
xs[::-1]         # [50, 40, 30, 20, 10]  reversed

One important detail: slicing creates a new list. xs[:] is, in fact, the idiomatic way to make a shallow copy of xs; we will return to its limits in Section 8.6. And one detail that is unique to lists, not strings: slice assignment lets you replace, insert, or delete an entire range in one statement.

Example 8.1Slice assignment, three useful tricks
xs = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]

# 1. Replace a range with a different number of items:
xs[1:4] = [99]              # [1, 99, 5]

# 2. Insert several items at once without insert():
xs = [1, 2, 3]
xs[1:1] = ["a", "b"]          # [1, 'a', 'b', 2, 3]

# 3. Clear a list in place, useful when other names point at it:
xs[:] = []                       # []  (same object, now empty)

The third form matters because it differs from xs = []: rebinding the name leaves the old list untouched and still visible through any other name that pointed at it. xs[:] = [] empties the same object in place, which every aliased name sees.

8.5Sorting, Properly

Python sorts with a custom algorithm called TimSort, designed by Tim Peters in 2002. It is a hybrid of insertion sort (fast for short runs and partially-sorted data) and merge sort (good asymptotic behaviour); it is stable, meaning items that compare equal keep their original relative order; and it is the default sort algorithm in Java, Android, and other places besides. Its average and worst case are O(n log n), and on data that is already nearly sorted it can approach O(n).

The two ways to invoke it are well worth distinguishing.

xs = [3, 1, 4, 1, 5]

xs.sort()                  # in-place: mutates xs, returns None
ys = sorted(xs)            # new list: leaves the argument unchanged, returns the sorted list

xs.sort(reverse=True)      # descending

For anything beyond plain values, the key argument is where sorting becomes a real tool. key=f means "decide each item's place by what f returns for it, not by the item itself". The function is called once per item (not once per comparison), so even an expensive key function is fine.

Example 8.3Sorting by a key, and by several
people = [
    {"name": "Ada",     "age": 28, "city": "London"},
    {"name": "Linus",   "age": 54, "city": "Helsinki"},
    {"name": "Caroline","age": 28, "city": "Paris"},
]

sorted(people, key=lambda p: p["age"])
# Ada, Caroline, Linus, ties broken by insertion order (stable sort)

# Sort by (age, name) to break ties on name:
sorted(people, key=lambda p: (p["age"], p["name"]))

# With operator.itemgetter, faster and cleaner than a lambda:
from operator import itemgetter
sorted(people, key=itemgetter("age", "name"))

Two facts to internalise. The sort is stable, so when two ages tie, the original order is preserved (Ada stays before Caroline in the first example because she was input first). And multi-field sorts are a tuple key: comparing tuples compares element by element, exactly as you want.

Stability, used well

Because Python's sort is stable, you can sort a list by several criteria by sorting multiple times, least significant first. To sort by age (primary) then name (secondary), one elegant way is: sort by name first, then by age. The age sort will preserve the name order for ties. This trick is not always the fastest, but it scales to any number of criteria without building a tuple key.

8.6Copy and the Aliasing Trap

Chapter 3 introduced the rule that names are labels on values, not boxes; b = a binds a second name to the same object. The list version of that rule is where it bites hardest, because lists are mutable and mutating one through one name is visible through the other.

a = [1, 2, 3]
b = a              # NOT a copy, b is the same list, with a second name
b.append(4)
print(a)           # [1, 2, 3, 4], a saw it too

To make an independent copy, you ask for one explicitly. Three forms all do the same thing for a flat list:

b = a.copy()         # the explicit method (Python 3.3+)
b = a[:]             # the slice idiom, older but still common
b = list(a)          # the constructor

Each of these makes a shallow copy: the outer list is new, but the items inside it are still the same objects as in the original. For a list of immutable values (numbers, strings, tuples) that is all you ever need. For a list that contains other mutable objects, especially other lists, shallow copying is not enough, because both the original and the copy still share the inner objects.

The remedy for nested mutable structures is the copy.deepcopy function, which copies all the way down. It is more expensive than a shallow copy, so reach for it only when you need it.

import copy
nested = [[1, 2], [3, 4]]
shallow = nested.copy()
deep    = copy.deepcopy(nested)

nested[0].append(99)
print(shallow)     # [[1, 2, 99], [3, 4]], shallow shares the inner lists
print(deep)        # [[1, 2],     [3, 4]], deep is independent all the way down

8.7Comprehensions in Depth

A list comprehension is a compact one-expression form for building a list out of another iterable. The basic shape is [expression for item in iterable]; with a filter, [expression for item in iterable if condition]. Read aloud it sounds like English ("the square of n, for each n in the range, if n is even"), and once it is in your fingers it becomes the most natural way to think about most transformations.

[x * x for x in range(10)]                       # [0, 1, 4, 9, ..., 81]
[x for x in range(10) if x % 2 == 0]            # [0, 2, 4, 6, 8]
[w.upper() for w in ["a", "b", "c"]]               # ['A', 'B', 'C']

The shape generalises in two directions that beginners often miss. Multiple for clauses nest: the leftmost iterates slowest, exactly as a series of nested loops would, and the values from all clauses are visible in the expression. Conditional expressions in the value position ((expr_true if cond else expr_false)) let you transform differently per item.

Example 8.4The five comprehension shapes
# 1. Transform every item
squares = [n * n for n in range(6)]
# [0, 1, 4, 9, 16, 25]

# 2. Filter
evens = [n for n in range(10) if n % 2 == 0]
# [0, 2, 4, 6, 8]

# 3. Branch per item (conditional in the expression position)
parity = ["even" if n % 2 == 0 else "odd" for n in range(5)]
# ['even', 'odd', 'even', 'odd', 'even']

# 4. Cartesian product (two for clauses)
pairs = [(x, y) for x in [1, 2] for y in ["a", "b"]]
# [(1, 'a'), (1, 'b'), (2, 'a'), (2, 'b')]

# 5. Flatten
flat = [x for row in [[1, 2], [3, 4]] for x in row]
# [1, 2, 3, 4]

Read multiple for clauses left to right, just as if they were nested. The fifth form, flattening, is worth memorising: it is the comprehension version of two nested loops appending into a single list.

Comprehensions are also faster than the equivalent for-and-append loop, often by a meaningful margin, because the bytecode is tighter and avoids the repeated attribute lookup on list.append. They are not magic, however; they are still O(n) in the size of the iterable, and a complicated expression inside the brackets is exactly as expensive as the equivalent code in a loop. The right question to ask before reaching for one is not "is it faster?" but "is it clearer?".

When NOT to use a comprehension

If the expression has side effects (printing, writing files, calling an API), use a plain for loop. Comprehensions are for building values; loops are for performing actions. A comprehension whose only purpose is to call print on each item is universally agreed to be ugly Python. The same goes for any comprehension that needs more than one nested if: at that point, an ordinary loop is clearer.

8.8Performance Pitfalls, and When Not to Use a List

The list is the right answer to most problems, but it is not the right answer to all problems, and the situations where it is wrong are predictable enough to be worth memorising. Four of them are common and avoidable.

1. Membership tests inside a loop

If you check whether a value is in a list, the cost is O(n). Doing that inside another loop is O(n²), and at scale it crawls. The fix is to put the values into a set, where membership is O(1).

# Slow: O(n * m), n items, each checked against an m-element list
banned = ["alice", "bob", ...]      # thousands
allowed = [u for u in users if u not in banned]

# Fast: O(n + m)
banned = set(["alice", "bob", ...])
allowed = [u for u in users if u not in banned]

2. Inserting or popping at the front in a loop

Every insert(0, x) or pop(0) shifts the entire list. If you find yourself doing this in a loop, the right container is collections.deque ("double-ended queue"), which gives you O(1) operations at both ends.

from collections import deque
queue = deque([1, 2, 3])
queue.appendleft(0)        # O(1) prepend
queue.popleft()             # O(1) pop from the front

3. Modifying a list while iterating over it

Adding to or removing from a list while a for loop walks over it leads to skipped items, infinite loops, or both. The fix is to iterate over a copy, or to build a new list with a comprehension.

# Buggy: skips an item every time one is removed
xs = [1, 2, 3, 4]
for x in xs:
    if x % 2 == 0:
        xs.remove(x)
print(xs)   # [1, 3, 4] , the 4 was missed!

# Fix: build a new list rather than mutate while iterating
xs = [x for x in xs if x % 2 != 0]

4. Building a long list by concatenation in a loop

The expression result = result + [x] builds a new list every iteration, copying everything so far. That makes the whole loop O(n²). Use append, which mutates in place at O(1) amortised, or build with a comprehension.

# Slow, O(n²)
result = []
for x in source:
    result = result + [x]

# Fast, O(n)
result = []
for x in source:
    result.append(x)

# Idiomatic, also O(n)
result = [x for x in source]
When to reach past the list

Use a set when order does not matter and membership does. Use a tuple when the data is fixed and you want to communicate that. Use a dict when you have key-to-value lookups. Use a deque when you push and pop at both ends. Use a numpy.ndarray when you need numerical work on a million-element array at near-C speed. The list is the default; the others exist for the cases where the default would mislead.

Tuples & Named Tuples

Tuples are not frozen lists; they are immutable records. Understanding that one distinction reshapes how you choose between the two, why a tuple can be a dictionary key, and why (5) is not a tuple but (5,) is.

Explicit is better than implicit. — Tim Peters, the Zen of Python (PEP 20)

The most useful sentence anyone ever wrote about the difference between lists and tuples is this: a list is a sequence of homogeneous things you intend to walk over, while a tuple is a record of heterogeneous fields you intend to address by position. A list of scores is a list, a row from a database is a tuple. The two types have similar surface syntax, similar indexing rules, and very different intent, and the experienced Python programmer chooses between them by intent, not by which one is "frozen". This chapter is about that distinction, the small set of language facts that make tuples work, and the named-tuple feature that gives positional records back their meaning.

9.1The Tuple, Properly Understood

A tuple is an ordered, immutable sequence of values, conventionally written in parentheses: (1, 2, 3). It looks like a list with rounder brackets, and on the surface it behaves like one: you index it, slice it, iterate over it, and ask its length the same way. The shared surface tempts beginners into thinking of a tuple as "a list you can't change", which works as a first approximation and slowly accumulates confusion afterwards.

The deeper way to read the type is one Guido van Rossum has stated repeatedly: tuples are for records, lists are for sequences. A list usually holds many things of the same kind, where every element plays the same role and only its position in the order matters. A tuple usually holds a few things of different kinds, where each position has its own meaning. [12, 7, 19, 4] is a list of temperatures; ("Ada", 28, "London") is a tuple that is one person's record. The fact that a tuple cannot be modified is a consequence of this intent rather than its purpose: a record's shape is fixed, so its type is too.

Tuple

An ordered, immutable sequence of values, type tuple. Written with parentheses (which are optional) and separated by commas (which are not). Tuples are hashable when every element is hashable, which lets them serve as dictionary keys and set members. Use them for records of fixed shape and for "multiple values returned together".

9.2The Comma Makes It a Tuple, Not the Parens

A small piece of syntax catches every newcomer once, and it is worth understanding now so you never trip on it. The parentheses do not make a tuple. The commas do. Parentheses serve their usual grouping role around an expression; when they enclose a comma-separated list of values, the commas are what mark the value as a tuple.

t = 1, 2, 3              # a tuple, no parens at all
t = (1, 2, 3)            # a tuple, parens added for clarity

p = (5)                  # NOT a tuple, just the number 5 in parentheses
type(p)                  # <class 'int'>

p = (5,)                 # a one-element tuple, the trailing comma makes it
type(p)                  # <class 'tuple'>

empty = ()                # the one exception: empty tuple needs parens
empty = tuple()           # or call the constructor

The empty tuple is the one case where parentheses cannot be omitted, because a comma with nothing on either side has no other meaning. Everywhere else, you can drop the parens; whether you do is a matter of style. Inside a function call, where the parentheses are already taken by the call itself, the trailing-comma rule is what disambiguates a one-element tuple from a parenthesised argument.

The classic slip

Writing f((5)) when you meant f((5,)) passes an int, not a one-element tuple. The same trap shows up in return statements: return (5) returns 5; return (5,) returns a one-tuple. If your function's "tuple of one thing" is being treated as the thing itself, look for a missing trailing comma.

9.3Packing and Unpacking

When several values appear on the right of an = separated by commas, Python packs them into a tuple. When several names appear on the left, Python unpacks a tuple into them. These two operations are the same machinery seen from opposite ends, and together they power some of the most idiomatic Python you will write.

# Packing: the right side becomes a tuple, which is bound to t.
t = 1, 2, 3
type(t), len(t)        # (<class 'tuple'>, 3)

# Unpacking: each name on the left gets one item from the right.
a, b, c = (1, 2, 3)
print(a, b, c)            # 1 2 3

# The swap is just simultaneous packing and unpacking:
a, b = b, a               # the right side packs to (b, a), the left unpacks it

The number of names on the left must match the number of values on the right, or Python raises ValueError with a precise count. To accept "the rest", whatever its size, use a starred name. The starred name collects everything not claimed by the other names, and the rule is that at most one star is allowed, but its position is free.

Example 9.1Starred unpacking, in three positions
first, *rest = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5)
print(first, rest)        # 1 [2, 3, 4, 5]

*head, last = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5)
print(head, last)         # [1, 2, 3, 4] 5

first, *middle, last = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5)
print(first, middle, last)  # 1 [2, 3, 4] 5

Two facts to notice. First, the starred name binds a list, not a tuple, even though the source was a tuple; the language chose list here because the result is the variable-length, "the rest" part of the destructure. Second, you can place the star anywhere in the target, and Python figures out which items belong to it by counting from both ends. The unpacking is greedy but unambiguous.

Unpacking works on any iterable, not just tuples; a, b, c = "abc" unpacks a string into three names. This generalisation, called iterable unpacking, is why for x, y in pairs: and for i, name in enumerate(names): read so cleanly: each iteration packs one element of the iterable, then unpacks it into the loop names.

9.4Records, Not Lists: When to Reach for a Tuple

The clearest way to know when a tuple is the right type is to ask one question: does each position have its own meaning? If yes, you are looking at a record, and a tuple is correct. If every position plays the same role, you have a sequence, and a list is correct. Three concrete cases sharpen the distinction.

DataRight typeWhy
A geographic coordinate (51.5, -0.1)tupleposition 0 is latitude, position 1 is longitude, different fields
The cities you visited on a triplistevery element is "a city"; order is the only structure
An RGB colour (255, 99, 71)tuplethree positions with three meanings (R, G, B)
The pixels of one row of an imagelistthousands of homogeneous values
The return value (name, age, city)tuplethree different fields packed for one transport
A queue of pending jobslist (or deque)every element is "a job"; you push and pop

One pragmatic consequence of choosing well: a tuple's immutability documents that a value's shape is fixed. A reader who sees def location(): return (lat, lon) can be confident that the result will always be a two-element record, while a reader who sees def location(): return [lat, lon] has no such guarantee, and any function the result is passed to could quietly extend it.

A tuple is also faster and lighter

Because a tuple's length is known at creation and never changes, CPython can store it more compactly than a list (no over-allocation, no separate header for length-vs-capacity, and the items can live in the same memory block). The constant-factor difference rarely matters for individual values, but for code that creates millions of small records it adds up. The right reason to choose a tuple is still semantic; the performance is a side benefit.

9.5Hashable: Compound Dictionary Keys

Chapter 3 introduced hashability and the rule that mutable objects (lists, dicts, sets) cannot be dictionary keys, while immutable ones (numbers, strings, tuples) can. Tuples are where this rule pays off, because a tuple of hashable items is itself hashable, and the resulting "compound key" lets a dictionary be indexed by structured data without inventing a wrapper.

locations = {
    (51.5, -0.1):  "London",
    (35.7, 139.7): "Tokyo",
    (40.7, -74.0): "New York",
}
locations[(51.5, -0.1)]    # 'London'

# A grid of pieces, keyed by (row, col):
board = {}
board[(0, 0)] = "rook"
board[(0, 4)] = "king"

# A list, attempted as a key, fails:
locations[[51.5, -0.1]]
# TypeError: unhashable type: 'list'

The reason a list cannot be a key, you will remember from Chapter 3.7, is the contract between hashing and equality: if a key's hash could change while it sat in the table, the table would lose track of where it had stored the entry. A list is mutable, so its hash is not allowed to exist at all; a tuple is immutable, so its hash is stable forever. The same rule lets tuples of hashable items be set members.

A tuple is hashable only if its elements are

A tuple containing a list is not hashable. The unhashability is contagious: any container that contains a non-hashable element is itself non-hashable, because computing the container's hash requires hashing each of its elements. If you find yourself wanting (name, friends) as a key where friends is a list, freeze the inner list into a tuple (or frozenset when order does not matter) first.

9.6Multiple Return Values

A function in Python returns exactly one value, but that value can be a tuple, and the calling code can unpack the tuple back into separate names in the same statement. The combination of packing on the way out and unpacking on the way in makes "multiple return values" feel as if Python supported them natively.

def min_max(xs):
    return min(xs), max(xs)         # packs into a tuple

lo, hi = min_max([3, 1, 4, 1, 5, 9])  # unpacks at the call site
print(lo, hi)                          # 1 9

The standard library uses this pattern throughout: divmod(a, b) returns (quotient, remainder), str.partition(sep) returns (before, sep, after), os.path.split(p) returns (head, tail). When you write your own function and the answer naturally has two or three parts, returning them as a tuple and letting the caller unpack is more idiomatic than packing them into a list or building a small ad-hoc class.

Example 9.2A function returns three things
def parse_email(addr):
    user, _, domain = addr.partition("@")
    local, _, tag = user.partition("+")
    return local, tag, domain

local, tag, domain = parse_email("ada+work@example.com")
print(local, tag, domain)   # ada work example.com

The function returns three values as a tuple; the caller takes the tuple apart in the same line. Notice that inside the function, str.partition itself returns a three-element tuple, which the local user, _, domain immediately unpacks. Tuples are the lingua franca of compound returns.

The convention of using _ as a name for "I do not care about this value" is widely understood and respected by linters. It is, of course, still a real variable, but the name signals to a reader "ignore this".

9.7Named Tuples: When Position Needs a Name

The one limitation of using bare tuples for records becomes obvious the moment a record grows beyond two or three fields. person[3] is not self-explanatory; whoever reads it has to remember which position is which, or look up the convention. The standard library has, since Python 2.6, offered a fix called the named tuple: a tuple with names for its positions. It costs nothing at runtime and reads as well as it works.

There are now two ways to declare one. The original is collections.namedtuple, a factory function. The modern one, since Python 3.5, is the class-based typing.NamedTuple, which adds type annotations and a familiar class syntax. Both produce the same kind of object.

Example 9.3The two named-tuple syntaxes
# Original: a factory call
from collections import namedtuple
Point = namedtuple("Point", ["x", "y"])

p = Point(3, 4)
p.x, p.y               # (3, 4), named access
p[0], p[1]           # (3, 4), positional access still works
x, y = p               # unpacking still works

# Modern: a class with type annotations
from typing import NamedTuple

class Point(NamedTuple):
    x: float
    y: float
    def distance_to_origin(self):
        return (self.x ** 2 + self.y ** 2) ** 0.5

p = Point(3, 4)
p.distance_to_origin()     # 5.0

The class-based form is the one to prefer in modern code. It documents types, allows methods, and reads like a small class. Behind the scenes both forms produce a subclass of tuple, which is why named tuples are still tuples in every sense: indexable, iterable, comparable, hashable when their fields are, and unpackable.

A handful of methods are added by the named-tuple machinery, all prefixed with an underscore to avoid colliding with field names. The three you will actually use:

MethodWhat it does
p._asdict()convert to a regular dict with the field names as keys
p._replace(x=10)return a new named tuple with one or more fields changed
Point._fieldsthe tuple of field names, useful for introspection

_replace is worth singling out, because it gives you a way to "update" an immutable record. Tuples cannot be modified, so the only way to get a new tuple with one field different is to create one; _replace does that, copying the other fields automatically.

p = Point(3, 4)
q = p._replace(x=10)         # Point(x=10, y=4), p is unchanged
Named tuple vs dataclass

If named tuples were not enough, Python 3.7 added @dataclass (PEP 557), which serves the same "data holder" role but with a mutable, regular-class body. The two are not redundant; they complement each other. Use a named tuple when the record is small, immutable, and frequently created or hashed (it is lighter and works as a dict key). Use a dataclass when the record has methods, will be mutated after creation, or has many fields that benefit from default values. We meet dataclasses properly in Chapter 19 (the modern-class chapter).

Solution 9.1: The comma test

Solution 9.2: Unpack the line

line = "Lovelace,Ada,1815,London"
last, first, *rest = line.split(",")
# last='Lovelace', first='Ada', rest=['1815', 'London']

# Star on the left captures the leading items:
*head, city = line.split(",")
# head=['Lovelace','Ada','1815'], city='London'

# Star in the middle splits front and back:
last, *middle, city = line.split(",")
# last='Lovelace', middle=['Ada','1815'], city='London'

The starred name always captures the variable-length portion, with the unstarred names taking from each end as needed. The starred result is always a list.

Solution 9.4: A compound dictionary key

board = {(0, 0): "rook", (0, 1): "knight", (0, 3): "queen"}
board[(0, 1)]         # 'knight'

board[[0, 4]] = "king"  # TypeError: unhashable type: 'list'

A list cannot be a dictionary key because it is mutable; its hash value is not allowed to exist at all. A tuple of hashable items is itself hashable and so makes a fine compound key.

Solution 9.5: Multiple return values

    return len(xs), statistics.mean(xs), statistics.stdev(xs)

print(n, m, s)         # 8 18 5.237...

The function returns a three-element tuple; the caller unpacks it into three names in one assignment. This is the idiomatic Python form of "multiple return values".

Solution 9.6: A named tuple

from typing import NamedTuple

class Person(NamedTuple):
    name: str
    age:  int
    city: str

people = [Person("Ada", 28, "London"),
          Person("Linus", 54, "Helsinki"),
          Person("Caroline", 35, "Paris")]

by_age = sorted(people, key=lambda p: p.age)
ada_in_paris = people[0]._replace(city="Paris")

The class-based form gives a type-annotated, methods-allowed declaration; sorting uses the same key= idiom as Chapter 8; _replace produces a new tuple with one field changed without mutating the original.

Solution 9.7: Hashability rules

(a) Yes, tuple of ints. (b) No: contains a list, which is unhashable; the unhashability is contagious. (c) Yes, tuple of str and int. (d) Yes, tuples nest fine. (e) No: a regular set is mutable and unhashable. (f) Yes: frozenset is the immutable, hashable counterpart of set, so the outer tuple is hashable too. The rule: a tuple is hashable iff every element is.

Solution 9.8: A design question

A bare tuple is the lightest but loses field meaning, so callers must remember the order, fine for two or three fields, but not eight. A dict gives named access at the cost of allowing arbitrary keys at runtime and no static checks. A typing.NamedTuple gives named access plus immutability plus type annotations, at near-zero cost, and works as a dictionary key. A @dataclass goes further by allowing mutation, default values, and behaviour (methods). For an eight-field profile that is mostly read, the choice is between NamedTuple and dataclass; pick NamedTuple if the profile is small, immutable, and frequently hashed or sent across function boundaries, and dataclass if the fields are likely to be updated in place or have many sensible defaults. Either is a strong choice over the bare tuple, and either is a clearer choice than a free-form dict.
